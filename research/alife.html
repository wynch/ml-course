Beat 1

Why can four rules make a glider?

Because the rules are applied to their own output. A shape that happens to recreate itself one cell over after four steps is a fixed point of "apply the rule four times, then shift", and fixed points persist. Persistence is the first property of anything we call alive.

John Conway's Game of Life (1970, made famous by Martin Gardner's column in Scientific American) is a grid of cells that are dead or alive. At every tick each cell counts its eight neighbours. A dead cell with exactly 3 live neighbours is born. A live cell with 2 or 3 survives. Everything else dies. In rule notation that is B3/S23. That is the whole physics.

What comes out is a zoo. Still lifes (a 2 × 2 block) are fixed points. Oscillators (the blinker, the pulsar) are cycles. Gliders are cycles that travel: the same five-cell shape reappears every 4 ticks, shifted one cell diagonally. In 1970 Bill Gosper found the glider gun, a finite pattern that emits a glider every 30 ticks forever, which settled Conway's question of whether a pattern could grow without bound. Gliders can carry bits and collide like logic gates, and Life was later shown to be Turing complete.

Change the numbers and most worlds die or boil. There are 218 "Life-like" rules (any subset of 0–8 for birth, any subset for survival). In 2024 the ASAL paper scored all of them for open-endedness with a vision foundation model, and Conway's rule landed in the top 5%. The interesting rules sit on a thin band between frozen and chaotic: the edge of chaos.

Beat 2

Why does a smooth Game of Life grow animals?

Because making space, time and states continuous turns "count the neighbours" into "measure the mass in a ring around you", and a blob whose own shape keeps its ring-mass in the sweet spot on one side and out of it on the other will keep growing at the front and dying at the back. That is locomotion.

Bert Chan's Lenia (2019) keeps Life's structure and smooths every part. Each cell holds a value between 0 and 1. The neighbourhood count becomes a convolution with a smooth ring-shaped kernel K of radius R. The birth/survival table becomes a smooth growth curve G, positive near a preferred neighbourhood value μ and negative elsewhere. Time advances in small steps dt.

At+dt = clip( At + dt · G(K ∗ At), 0, 1 )
G(u) = 2·exp( −(u − μ)² / 2σ² ) − 1 K ∗ A is the weighted mass in a ring around each cell. G says: grow if that mass is close to μ (within about σ), decay otherwise.

With the right numbers you get Orbium, the creature below: a crescent that glides at constant speed, survives collisions with small debris and has a front and a back. Chan catalogued hundreds of such species with names, families and "anatomy". The descendants push in two directions. Flow-Lenia (Plantec et al., 2023 to 2025) conserves mass, so matter is moved around instead of created, creatures become common, and the parameters travel with the matter, so different species share one world and evolve. Particle Lenia (2023) replaces the grid with particles that move down an energy landscape.

The cost is one convolution per step. Here it is done with a 2-D FFT on a 128 × 128 torus, which is why it runs in real time.

Beat 3

Why can a tissue regrow what you cut off?

Because the shape is stored in a rule every cell follows, so every cell still knows it after damage. If cells grow only where a signal says "the body continues here", then removing tissue removes the signal's stop condition and growth restarts until the shape is back.

Growing Neural Cellular Automata (Mordvintsev, Randazzo, Niklasson and Levin, Distill 2020) made this a learning problem. Each cell has 16 channels (colour, an "alive" alpha and 12 hidden channels). It perceives its 3 × 3 neighbourhood through fixed Sobel filters, and a tiny shared network of about 8,000 parameters outputs an update. Cells fire at random times, so nothing is synchronised. Trained to grow an emoji from one seed, the first version grew it and then kept growing into a mess. Training from a pool of past states made the pattern stable, and training on damaged states made it regenerate: cut a lizard's tail and the cells regrow it.

The lab uses a hand-designed rule instead of trained weights (we cannot ship trained NCA weights here, and the hand rule makes the mechanism readable). One organiser cell emits a signal. The signal diffuses through living tissue and decays, so it falls with distance. A dead cell next to tissue comes alive if its neighbour's signal is above a threshold. A living cell whose signal falls well below that threshold dies. A living cell that finds itself at a local peak of a fading signal becomes a new organiser. That is Lewis Wolpert's positional information with growth and death added.

Why this counts as cognition for Levin. Michael Levin (Tufts) argues in TAME (2022) that intelligence is competence at reaching a goal in some space by more than one path, and that cells and tissues do this in "morphospace". Evidence he points to: frog (Xenopus) skin cells freed from the embryo assemble into xenobots (Kriegman et al., 2020) that swim and, in later work, gather loose cells into new xenobots; adult human airway cells form anthrobots (2023) that move on their own. Even a classic sorting algorithm, run as a distributed array of cells with some broken ones, sorts around the damage and shows side goals nobody programmed (Zhang, Goldstein and Levin, 2024).

A building where each brick carries the blueprint and checks its neighbours: knock out a wall and the bricks at the hole start laying new bricks until the wall is back. That is cheaper than a foreman, and it survives the foreman being shot.

CarefulThe lab rule is hand-written: it shows that local rules can store and restore a shape. Growing NCA learn such rules by gradient descent through many simulation steps, and their hidden channels play the role our single signal plays here.
Beat 4

Why do self-copying programs appear when nobody asks for them?

Because in a world where programs can write onto each other, a program that copies itself onto its neighbour turns that neighbour into another copier. Copying compounds and everything else does not, so the first accidental copier, however crude, takes over. No fitness function is needed: persistence through copying is the fitness.

"Computational Life" (Agüera y Arcas, Alakuijala, Evans, Laurie, Mordvintsev, Niklasson, Randazzo and Versari, 2024) built a primordial soup out of code. Each "organism" is a 64-byte tape in BFF, a Brainfuck variant where the program and its data are the same bytes. Ten byte values are instructions: move head 0 (< >), move head 1 ({ }), change a byte (+ -), copy between the heads (. ,) and loop ([ ]). The other 246 values do nothing. Each epoch the soup is shuffled into random pairs; each pair is glued into one 128-byte tape, run for up to 8,192 steps, and split back. That is the entire world. There is no reward and no selection.

For thousands of epochs nothing seems to happen. Then, suddenly, the soup fills with copies of a few programs, and its high-order entropy jumps: the bytes are as varied as before, but the soup becomes highly compressible because the same code is everywhere. The authors saw replicators arise even with no background mutation at all, which is why Agüera y Arcas argues (MLST 2025-10 and 2026-02) that merging, or symbiogenesis, drives jumps in complexity more than mutation does. The ancestors are 1990s digital-life systems like Tom Ray's Tierra (1991) and Avida, where the first replicator was written by hand.

Beat 5

Why ask a foundation model what looks alive?

Because "interesting" and "lifelike" were judged by human eyes, which do not scale to billions of rules. A vision foundation model has already learned a space where similar-looking things are close, so novelty, diversity and "looks like a cell dividing" become distances you can optimise.

ASAL, Automating the Search for Artificial Life with Foundation Models (Akarsh Kumar, Chris Lu, Louis Kirsch, Yujin Tang, Kenneth Stanley, Phillip Isola and David Ha; Sakana AI and MIT, Dec 2024; Artificial Life 2025 and Best Oral at ALIFE 2025) renders simulations to images, embeds them with CLIP (DINOv2 as an ablation), and searches the simulator's parameters in three modes. It ran on Boids, Particle Life, Life-like CAs, Lenia (through the Leniabreeder code) and NCA.

The next step: the model proposes the targets. ASAL++ (Baid et al., Sept 2025) gives a second foundation model (Gemma-3) the visual history of a Lenia run and asks it for the next target, again and again. The result is evolutionary trajectories that keep getting more complex. The foundation model is now the driver of open-endedness, not only its judge.

Kumar has since turned the same substrates toward other questions: a Red Queen arms race of LLM-written programs in Core War (Digital Red Queen, 2026), and NCA trajectories as pretraining data for language models (beat 6).

This project's bridge ideaSwap CLIP for a JEPA encoder as ASAL's judge, run it on Lenia or NCA, and compare which creatures each finds. A JEPA judges by predictability in latent space: high surprise means "this went somewhere my model of the world did not expect", and a falling surprise over time means "but I can learn it". Novel plus learnable is the Hughes and Rocktäschel definition of open-endedness. A small simulator, a frozen V-JEPA or LeJEPA encoder and an evolution strategy fit on a laptop. It is listed under bridge 2 in the fields map and in chapter 17.
Beat 6

Why should people who train big models care about cellular automata?

Because three bridges opened in 2025 and 2026: NCA dynamics turned out to be good pretraining data for language models, tiny NCAs solve some abstract reasoning puzzles, and artificial life is where open-endedness can be measured cheaply.

BridgeResultWhy it matters
Training LMs via NCA
Lee, Han, Kumar, Agrawal · 2026-03		"Pre-pre-training" on about 164M tokens of NCA trajectories improves later language modelling perplexity by up to 6% and speeds convergence up to 1.6×; it beats pre-pre-training on 1.6B tokens of C4. Attention layers carry most of the transfer.Structure first, content later: a cheap, tunable source of structure. Code prefers simpler NCA dynamics, web text and math more complex ones.

Xu & Miikkulainen · 2025-06		Per-task NCAs of about 10k parameters, trained in minutes, solve 23 of 172 feasible ARC-AGI-1 training tasks.Developmental, local computation as a route to abstraction (see chapter 09).

Jain, Reimers, Nichele · 2026-09		Each pixel holds a tiny network; cells survive by cloning a living neighbour's network with mutation. The substrate persists in 20 of 24 long runs.Diversity metrics for open-ended substrates: a trade-off between phenotypic and genotypic diversity.
Flow-Lenia

The project's second bridge idea follows directly: a predictive NCA, trained with a JEPA-style latent prediction loss instead of pixel targets. Growing NCA are trained to match an image; a predictive NCA would be trained so that each cell's hidden state predicts its neighbourhood's next hidden state. Nobody has published that yet as far as the project's notes know.

The open question for the whole field: can any substrate produce unbounded novelty, and how would we measure it? There is no accepted metric. That is the subject of the next chapter.

Three things to remember

  1. A creature is a fixed point that moves.Gliders and Orbium are shapes the rule keeps rebuilding one step over. Most of rule space freezes or boils; life sits at the edge.
  2. The blueprint lives in every cell.Cut a tissue governed by a local rule and the rule regrows it, because the stop condition for growth is gone.
  3. Copying is its own reward.In a soup of random programs, the first accidental copier takes over without any fitness function, and evolution starts right after.
