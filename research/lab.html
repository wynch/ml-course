Beat 1

Why is a 16 GB laptop enough to say something new?

Because compute spent on the problem at hand keeps beating compute spent memorising everything in advance, and because the open questions here are about small models: how a 5.5M-parameter encoder's geometry survives 4 bits is a question you can only answer at small scale.

The evidence for "small and efficient keeps catching up" is broad. On abstraction: TRM (7M parameters), CompressARC (76K), neural cellular automata for ARC (about 10K per task). On world models: LeWM (15M) plans 48× faster than DINO-WM. On the edge: Gemma 3n and Gemma 4 E2B/E4B run offline on phones, EmbeddingGemma 2 in 191 MB of RAM, BitNet b1.58 with ternary weights. The convergent explanation, in Sara Hooker's terms: FLOPs are a poor proxy for capability. The Bitter Lesson is about general methods using compute, and test-time search is one of them.

Vinz's machine is an Apple M5 laptop with 16 GB of unified memory and no discrete GPU. PyTorch reaches its GPU through MPS. The field map rates each of its 23 fields for this exact machine:

Beat 2

Why start with quantization, of all things?

Because it scores high on every axis at once: it tests a specific theory prediction, it is about the edge, nobody has published it, and it fits on the laptop. Change what you value and the order changes; that is the point of the ranker below.

Its recommendation: warm up with the theory notebooks (#7), then do quantization (#1) plus the edge benchmark (#2) as one project. Beat 3

Why run three tiny notebooks before the real experiment?

Because each one turns a theory claim into a number you can check in a minute, and the second one produced the reason for the third: a plain deep JEPA is anisotropic by design, which is exactly what quantization punishes.

Notebook 1 · done 2026-10-09 · ~1 min CPU

Drive vs. decay: which directions escape collapse

Linear JEPA, fixed predictor W_p = αI, EMA target, context keep probability p and target keep probability p_tgt, minibatch SGD on a 32-d stationary signal.

μk = (ptgt/α) · λk / (p·λk + (1−p)·d)Own closed form for constant-diagonal covariance d. Mode k escapes collapse iff μk > 1.
  • Predicted vs measured early growth rates agree within 0.008 across all 32 modes; the escaping-mode count matches at 14 of 16 settings (the misses sit within 2% of μ = 1).
  • Context masking is a spectral filter: it favours modes with more than average variance. Without it, collapse is all or nothing.
  • The EMA rate changes speed, not outcome: 3 orders of magnitude never flipped a mode.
Notebook 2 · done 2026-10-10 · ~1 min CPU

Implicit bias: why depth makes JEPA picky

Littwin et al.'s deep linear ODE, then minibatch SGD on finite networks.

  • MAE learns the louder feature first at every depth. JEPA's preference for predictable features grows with depth: a quiet feature (ρ 0.9) overtook one with twice the covariance but ρ 0.4 only at depth 5 to 7.
  • SGD reaches the paper's fixed points: ρL for JEPA, ρL/(L+1) for MAE.
  • Own consequence: a JEPA embedding's scale spread grows like (ρmax/ρmin)L, while MAE's stays bounded. A plain deep JEPA is anisotropic by design. That is the motivation for testing SIGReg under quantization.
Notebook 3 · CPU pilot done 2026-10-10 · ~4 min

Does SIGReg quantize better?

8 × 8 digits, MLP encoder, 16-d embedding, 3 seeds per method. Points lost after quantization:

SIGReg5.011.81.25.30.42

  • Kurtosis tracked embedding loss better than condition number (VICReg: 4,275 yet mild loss). The mechanism is the lab in chapter 12.
Beat 4

Why did the first real run stop after 30 minutes?

Because the battery was dying. A ViT-Tiny JEPA keeps the M5's GPU busy for hours, and on battery that drains the laptop. The fix was a lighter run (about 5 GB) that saves a checkpoint every epoch, with the Mac plugged in overnight.

The real test of build idea #1 is experiments/quant-vit/jepa_quant.py: a ViT-Tiny (5.5M parameters, 4 × 4 patches on 32 × 32 images, an 8 × 8 token grid) trained I-JEPA-style on CIFAR-100 with SIGReg, VICReg or an EMA teacher, 100 epochs each, 2 seeds. After training, a linear probe is fitted on full-precision embeddings, then the weights (W8/W4/W3, per output channel) or the output embedding (E8/E4/E3, per tensor) are quantized. It also records condition number, effective rank, kurtosis, channel ratio and residual-stream outlier ratios.

Each run saves runs/<method>_s<seed>.ckpt after every epoch, written atomically so a kill mid-save cannot corrupt it. Rerunning the same command resumes, so an interruption costs at most one epoch.

Gotcha in the scriptA checkpoint remembers its batch size and epoch count. Resuming with a different --batch or --epochs is refused ("delete it or match those"), because the learning-rate schedule and step count would no longer line up. Switching to the lighter batch means starting that run over.

Beat 5

Why write down the losing result before the numbers arrive?

Because once the table exists, every outcome can be told as a story. Fixing in advance which numbers would count as "SIGReg quantizes better", which would count against it, and how big a difference must be to beat seed noise, is what makes the run a test instead of a demo.

The hypothesis

Encoders trained with SIGReg lose less accuracy under low-bit post-training quantization than VICReg- or EMA-trained encoders, because their embeddings are closer to an isotropic Gaussian: lighter tails and a lower condition number.

The hypothesis has two halves, and the card tests them separately. The effect: SIGReg loses fewer points at 4 and 3 bits. The mechanism: SIGReg's embedding geometry is the reason, so the method ordering by geometry should match the ordering by loss. Getting the effect without the mechanism is a different finding ("SIGReg wins for another reason"), and the pilot already flagged which geometry to trust: kurtosis, not condition number.

Beat 6

Why does every next experiment start from the same checkpoints?

Because the six trained encoders are the expensive part, and several open questions only need to measure them: whether isotropy predicts internal factoring, how fast they run on real hardware, and whether an evolved regularizer could beat SIGReg.

reuses the checkpoints · no training

Isotropic, or also factored?

Landscape idea 1: FER-style weight sweeps, Shai et al.'s orthogonal-factor test, mutual-kNN alignment with DINO, next to the quantization loss. Does a global property (isotropy) predict an internal one (factoring)? Chapter 07.

reuses the checkpoints · export only

Edge benchmark

Build idea #2: export the quantized encoders to ONNX and Core ML, measure latency, peak memory and energy on the laptop and a phone. Theory predicts which survive compression; hardware says what that buys.

reuses notebook 3 · CPU

Evolve the regularizer

Landscape idea 6: OpenEvolve or ShinkaEvolve with a small local model mutates the anti-collapse term, scored by linear probe plus INT4 robustness on the CPU pilot. A verifier-driven search over the exact thing under study (chapter 09).

new direction · hours on MPS

A JEPA world model for ARC-AGI-3

Landscape idea 4: a LeWM-style JEPA learns one or two ARC-AGI-3 games from random play and plans in latent space. The 2026 winners used code world models; nobody seems to have tried a JEPA there.

The five bridges out of JEPA

The field map ends with five places where JEPA meets a neighbouring field. All five are marked laptop-sized, and all five are in the ranker above:

  1. Sparse autoencoders on I-JEPA features (interpretability): what dictionary does a non-generative encoder learn? See chapter 08.
  2. JEPA as the interestingness judge in open-ended search: swap CLIP for a JEPA encoder in ASAL and compare which Lenia or NCA creatures it finds (chapter 14).
  3. Predictive NCA: train a neural cellular automaton with a latent-prediction objective instead of pixel targets (chapter 13).
  4. SSM or liquid predictor for V-JEPA: a linear-time predictor aimed at edge video.
  5. Active inference vs JEPA, side by side on one toy environment. The SIGReg free-energy paper already connects them.
Theory crossed with practiceThe landscape survey's table pairs each theory result with what it unlocks. Three rows point straight at this chapter: isotropic Gaussian embeddings are optimal (LeJEPA) → possibly quantization-friendly encoders, our experiment; JEPA pretraining error bounds planning regret (Cui et al., 2026) → choose an edge world model's latent size on principle; over-squashing and softmax dispersion → awareness that quantization worsens attention collapse.

Three things to remember

  1. Small questions need small machines.How a 5.5M-parameter encoder survives 4 bits can only be answered at small scale. A 16 GB laptop is the right instrument, not a compromise.
  2. Write the losing result first.A pre-registered margin and a refutation criterion turn the ViT run into a test. Effect and mechanism are checked separately.
  3. Plug it in and checkpoint every epoch.The first run died with the battery after 30 minutes. Atomic per-epoch checkpoints mean the next interruption costs one epoch, not one night.
