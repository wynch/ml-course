Why does JEPA ignore the noise?

gradient descent does not learn every feature at once, and the two objectives rank features differently. A masked autoencoder learns the loudest feature first (largest covariance). A JEPA learns the most predictable feature first (largest regression coefficient), and the deeper its encoder, the stronger that preference. Noise is loud but unpredictable, so a deep JEPA reaches it last and keeps it small.

Beat 1

Why would a network learn one feature before another?

Because from a small initialization, each feature grows at a speed set by the data's statistics along that feature. Whatever grows fastest is learned first and shapes the rest. The objective decides which statistic sets the speed.

Train a deep linear network from tiny weights and it does not fit the data all at once. It picks up one direction, plateaus, picks up the next. Saxe and colleagues described these stage-like dynamics for supervised deep linear nets in 2014. The order is the network's implicit bias: two solutions with the same loss can be reached in different orders, and in a finite training run the order decides what you end up with.

Littwin and colleagues at Apple (NeurIPS 2024) worked out that order for two self-supervised objectives on the same deep linear encoder W̄ = WL⋯W1, with a view x and a target view y. Give each feature two numbers:

  • λ, the covariance between the feature in the two views: how loud it is.
  • ρ = λ / σ², the regression coefficient: how much of the feature in one view is predicted by the other. ρ = 1 means no noise, ρ near 0 means mostly noise. This is how predictable it is.
MAE: ½ E‖ V W̄ x − y ‖² JEPA: ½ E‖ V W̄ x − sg(W̄) y ‖²The only difference: MAE regresses the raw target view; JEPA regresses the target through the same encoder, with a stop-gradient (sg). That self-reference is where the extra preference for ρ comes from.

With diagonal covariances and the paper's balanced initialization (orthogonal layers of scale ε1/L), the size w of each feature inside W̄ obeys its own scalar equation:

JEPA: dw/dt = λ · w3 − 1/L − (λ/ρ) · w³
MAE: dw/dt = λ · w2 − 1/L − (λ/ρ) · w³The first term drives growth, the second stops it at the fixed point. Early on w is tiny and the first term dominates. In MAE the start-up speed is set by λ alone. In JEPA the exponent is one higher, so a feature crawls until it has some size, and the ρ in the brake term matters sooner. The paper shows the resulting preference for high ρ grows with depth L.

Beat 2

Why does a deeper JEPA get pickier?

Because each extra layer raises the power of w in the growth term, which slows every feature's start and gives predictability more time to matter. In this project's run of the paper's ODE, a quiet clean feature overtook a feature with twice the covariance only at depth 5 to 7.

This projectNotebook 2 (jepa/notebooks/02-implicit-bias.ipynb) integrates exactly these ODEs. Its conclusion: "The ordering effect is real but gradual. It only overturns a 2x covariance gap with a fairly deep encoder." The lab above recomputes the same curves in your browser with an adaptive step.
Beat 3

Where does training end up, and does real SGD agree?

Because the brake term stops growth where the two terms balance: at w = ρL for JEPA and w = ρL/(L+1) for MAE. The JEPA endpoint shrinks noisy features exponentially in depth. Minibatch SGD on finite networks lands on both formulas within a few percent.

Set dw/dt = 0. For JEPA, λw3−1/L = (λ/ρ)w³ gives w1/L = ρ, so w∞ = ρL. For MAE, w1+1/L = ρ gives ρL/(L+1). Notice that λ cancels in both: in the end, only predictability decides how big a feature is. Loudness only decided when it got there.

Take ρ = 0.4 and depth 3. MAE keeps the feature at 0.43/4 ≈ 0.50. JEPA keeps it at 0.4³ = 0.064. A clean feature with ρ = 0.95 stays at 0.86 in both. So the JEPA does not delete noise, it turns it down by a factor that compounds per layer.

Beat 4

Why can "predictable" be a trap?

Because predictable is not the same as meaningful. Noise that changes slowly is extremely predictable from one frame to the next. A JEPA rewarded for predictability will happily encode a slow distractor and ignore the moving object you cared about.

Sobal and colleagues (2022) trained VICReg-style JEPAs on simple worlds of moving dots and found they latch onto slow-varying features, including slow noise distractors. The implicit bias of Beat 2 is the same mechanism seen from the other side: if what the objective ranks by is "how well can the next view be regressed from this one", then a background pattern that barely changes wins, whatever it means.

The lab is a toy model of that setting. A dot wanders in a box. Behind it, a noise texture drifts at a speed you set, and a fast pixel flicker sits on top. Each candidate feature is scored the way a JEPA with a linear predictor and a variance constraint scores it: prediction error from the previous frame, divided by the feature's variance (the variance term forbids cheating by shrinking).

CarefulThis is a scoring toy: features are scored one at a time with the exact statistic a linear JEPA ranks by. Sobal et al. trained real networks; their paper, not this lab, is the evidence that real JEPAs fall in. What the lab shows is why they would.
Beat 5

Why do JEPAs on time series sort data into regimes nobody labeled?

Because the most predictable functions of a dynamical system are the ones that barely change under its dynamics, and for a system that switches between regimes those are the regime indicators. A predictability-loving objective finds them.

The Koopman operator of a dynamical system acts on functions of the state, f ↦ f∘step. Its eigenfunctions with eigenvalue near 1 are almost invariant: they keep their value step after step. For a system with metastable regimes (a weather pattern, a machine's operating mode), the function "which regime am I in" is such an eigenfunction.

Ruiz-Morales and colleagues (AAAI 2026) show that, under an idealized JEPA loss, optimal encoders span exactly these regime indicator functions. Many entangled optima exist, and a near-identity linear predictor is what selects the interpretable one. That explains the emergent clustering seen in time-series JEPAs. abstract only

It also reframes Beat 4. A slow noise texture is a near-invariant function too. The objective cannot tell a meaningful invariant from a meaningless one; what you feed it decides which kind exists.

Ranked by MAE

Covariance λ

Loud first. Pixels with big variance (texture, lighting) dominate early training. Balestriero and LeCun (2024): reconstruction spends capacity on high-variance subspaces that matter little for perception.

Ranked by JEPA

Regression coefficient ρ

Predictable first, more so with depth. Noise is turned down by ρL. Slow invariants (regimes, but also slow distractors) win.

Both ignore

What the data does not vary

Neither objective can find a feature the views never disagree or agree on. Masking and view choice set which ρ each feature has: the curriculum lives in the data pipeline.

Beat 6

Why does the same bias make a JEPA hard to compress?

Because turning noise down by ρL makes the embedding's channel scales spread like (ρmax/ρmin)L. Low-bit quantization uses one step size for the whole tensor, so the tiny channels round to zero.

This consequence is the project's own, not a claim in Littwin et al. At the fixed points, the largest-to-smallest feature ratio is (ρmax/ρmin)L for JEPA, which grows exponentially with depth, and (ρmax/ρmin)L/(L+1) for MAE, which never exceeds ρmax/ρmin. A plain deep JEPA is anisotropic by design. That matters for running a JEPA on a device, and it is why the next chapter's isotropic regularizer should help.

Three things to remember

  1. MAE ranks by loudness, JEPA by predictability.Covariance λ sets MAE's learning order; the regression coefficient ρ increasingly sets JEPA's as the encoder gets deeper.
  2. Noise is turned down by ρ to the L.The JEPA fixed point is ρL, so unpredictable features shrink exponentially with depth, and real SGD lands there.
  3. Predictable is not meaningful.Slow distractors and regime indicators are both near-invariant. The same bias that skips noise also squeezes channel scales apart, which low-bit quantization punishes.
