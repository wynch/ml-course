Why an isotropic Gaussian?

an encoder is trained before anyone knows the downstream task, and the shape that does least damage to the worst task is a round cloud with equal spread in every direction. LeJEPA proves this, then enforces it cheaply by checking random 1-D shadows of the embedding against a bell curve. The same target also makes latent world models identifiable and turns the loss into a variational free energy.

Beat 1

Why is a round cloud the safest embedding for tasks you have not seen?

Because a downstream probe pays most on the direction the embedding squashed. With the total spread fixed, the smallest direction is as large as it can be exactly when all directions are equal. Isotropy maximizes the worst case.

After pretraining, an encoder gets judged by probes: a linear classifier or regressor trained on a few labeled examples of a task nobody told the encoder about. Suppose a task's answer lives along a direction where the embedding has tiny variance s. To get a unit of signal out of that direction, the probe needs a weight of size 1/√s. A regularized probe shrinks large weights, so it underfits there (bias). An unregularized one estimates that weight from a direction with almost no signal, so it is noisy (variance).

Balestriero and LeCun (LeJEPA, 2025) turn this into a theorem: anisotropy amplifies both the bias and the variance of linear probes, and the isotropic Gaussian is the embedding distribution that minimizes worst-case downstream risk. Their result also covers nonlinear probes such as k-NN, which is where "Gaussian" and not merely "equal variances" comes in. The lab simulates the linear case.

worst task ∝ 1/smin, with s1 + … + sd = d
⟹ best: s1 = … = sd = 1Fixing the total variance is the fair comparison: any encoder can be rescaled, so only the shape counts. Under that budget, the minimum eigenvalue is largest when the spectrum is flat.
Beat 2

Why can you test a 1,000-dimensional distribution with 1-D shadows?

Because of the Cramér–Wold theorem: a distribution is fully determined by all its 1-D projections. So "is the embedding an isotropic Gaussian?" becomes "is every shadow a standard bell curve?", and a few random shadows per batch are a cheap, unbiased spot check.

Matching a full high-dimensional density is hopeless at batch size 256. Matching moments is cheap but blind: VICReg pushes each dimension's variance to 1 and the off-diagonal covariances to 0, which fixes the second moment and nothing else. SIGReg (Sketched Isotropic Gaussian Regularization) does this instead:

  1. Draw K random unit directions ak (LeJEPA resamples them every step).
  2. Project the batch: p = Z ak, one number per sample.
  3. Compare the empirical characteristic function of p with that of N(0, 1) using the Epps–Pulley statistic, and average over directions.
EP = N · ∫ | (1/N) Σj ei t pj − e−t²/2 |² · e−t²/2 dt
SIGReg = mean over k of EP(Z ak)Cost is linear in batch size and in K; there are no pairwise distances and no matrix inverses. The integral is a sum over a small grid of t (this project's notebook uses 33 points on [−5, 5]). Total loss: (1 − w)·prediction + w·SIGReg, with one trade-off weight w.

Because the target is a fixed distribution with unit spread, collapse is not a quiet minimum any more: a collapsed embedding casts a spike as its shadow, and EP is huge. That is why LeJEPA can drop the stop-gradient, the EMA teacher and the other heuristics from chapter 03.

Beat 3

Why would a Gaussian target let a JEPA recover the true state of the world?

Because if the hidden state is Gaussian and evolves with additive noise, then an encoder whose output is forced to be Gaussian and predictable can only differ from the truth by a linear map. Klindt, LeCun and Balestriero (2026) report that the Gaussian is the only latent law for which this holds.

Good probe accuracy does not mean the encoder found the real variables. Many embeddings predict equally well. Identifiability asks when the objective forces the recovered latents to match the true ones, up to a harmless ambiguity.

When does LeJEPA learn a world model? The setting: true latent states with stationary dynamics plus additive noise, observed through an unknown nonlinear mixing (pixels). The abstract states that LeJEPA then recovers the latents up to a linear map, that the Gaussian is the only latent law for which this works, that recovery degrades gracefully when the assumptions are off, and that this enables optimal planning in the latent space. abstract only

An intuition (ours, not the paper's proof): independent component analysis has the mirror-image result. ICA can undo a linear mix of sources unless they are Gaussian, because a rotated Gaussian looks identical. LeJEPA asks for less (a linear map, not the exact axes), and here the Gaussian's rotation symmetry is the leftover freedom rather than an obstacle.

Passive prediction

Up to a linear map

Klindt et al. 2026: Gaussian latents with additive-noise dynamics, LeJEPA objective. A linear probe can read the true state out. abstract only

With actions

Up to permutation and per-component transforms

Liu, Huang, Shi 2026, "I Act Therefore I Am": prediction accuracy alone does not imply correct latents. A likelihood plus entropy objective identifies the causal state variables, up to component-wise transforms and permutation, if and only if actions induce enough variation across the transition mechanisms. Their instance is called A-JEPA. abstract only

Picture it

You watch shadows of a juggler on a wall. Many different jugglers cast the same shadows. If you also know the balls fly on simple parabolas with a bit of wind (stationary dynamics, additive noise), and you insist your reconstruction moves the same way, the only jugglers left are the real one seen from a different angle: a linear map away. Being allowed to push a ball (actions) narrows it further, to the real juggler up to relabeling.

Beat 4

Why does SIGReg look like a variational free energy?

Because a free energy is "prediction error minus entropy of your beliefs", and SIGReg's job is to keep the embedding's entropy at its maximum for a fixed spread. Read that way, JEPA plus SIGReg is a variational objective, and the other anti-collapse terms are looser or one-sided bounds on the same entropy.

Among all distributions with a given covariance, the Gaussian has the highest entropy. So "make the embedding an isotropic Gaussian with unit variance" is the same as "make its entropy as large as it can be at that scale". Collapse is the opposite: entropy going to minus infinity.

Two 2026 papers make the link formal. Var-JEPA (Gögl, Yau) shows the encoder and predictor match the variational posterior and a learned conditional prior of a coupled latent-variable model; ordinary JEPA is its deterministic special case, and a single ELBO removes the need for ad hoc anti-collapse terms while giving latent uncertainty. Arnez and Gomez-Villa read SIGReg as the entropy term of a variational free energy and rank regularizers by how they bound the latent entropy. Both abstract only.

The same paper notes current JEPA world models miss a state-epistemic term, which is the bridge to Beat 5.

Beat 5

Why would an agent minimizing free energy go looking for information?

Because when free energy is projected into the future, it splits into risk (expected outcomes far from preferred ones) and ambiguity (outcomes that would not tell you the state). Minimizing ambiguity is curiosity. It comes out of the same objective with no exploration bonus added.

Karl Friston's free-energy principle says any system that persists must keep minimizing variational free energy, a bound on surprise, under a generative model of what causes its sensations. Perception, learning and action are all inference. To plan, an active-inference agent scores each policy by its expected free energy:

G(a) = KL[ q(o | a) ‖ C(o) ] + Eq(s | a) H[ p(o | s) ]
= risk + ambiguity
= − (expected log preference) − (expected information gain)C(o) is a preferred-outcome distribution: the agent's goals, written as beliefs. The second line is how Friston usually writes it; the third shows the same quantity as "pragmatic value" plus "epistemic value", both to be maximized.

The lab is the classic T-maze. A reward sits in the left or right arm. Arms pay 75 percent when correct and 25 percent when wrong, and entering an arm ends the trial. A cue at the bottom tells you which arm is correct (with a reliability you set). Two greedy agents look one step ahead: one minimizes G, the other maximizes expected reward only.

Where JEPA meets Friston

LeCun's JEPA world model plus a cost module plus model-predictive planning is structurally close to Friston's generative model plus expected-free-energy planning. The project's field notes list the differences: JEPA is non-generative and energy-based, not a likelihood; active inference is fully Bayesian and builds epistemic value into planning. SIGReg as free energy and Var-JEPA as an ELBO narrow the first gap. The missing state-epistemic term, flagged by Arnez and Gomez-Villa, is the second.

The disagreement is live. Friston argues on MLST that intelligence is accumulating evidence for one's world model and that curiosity comes "for free". Mattick (MLST, 2026-09) argues prediction is not control. Bengio argues for building the predictor deliberately without agency. See the Why Atlas map, question 13.

Three things to remember

  1. Round protects the worst task.With total variance fixed, equal variances maximize the smallest one, which is where an unseen task would hurt most. The average task barely cares.
  2. Every shadow must be a bell.Cramér–Wold turns a high-dimensional test into random 1-D ones. The X shape passes VICReg and fails SIGReg.
  3. Max entropy is a free energy.The Gaussian is the maximum-entropy law at fixed spread, which makes SIGReg an entropy term, the latents identifiable up to a linear map, and curiosity a short step away.
