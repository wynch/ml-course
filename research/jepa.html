Why predict in latent space?

the world is full of detail you cannot predict, and a model that has to paint every pixel wastes itself on that detail. A JEPA predicts a summary of the hidden part instead, and the summary is allowed to leave the noise out.

Beat 1

Why not just predict the pixels?

Because pixel loss counts every leaf and every speck of sensor noise as much as the object that matters, so the model spends its capacity where the variance is, not where the meaning is.

Imagine covering half of a photo of a dog in a park and asking someone to fill it in. A person says "the rest of the dog, some grass, a tree". A model trained with a pixel loss has to say which blades of grass, at which brightness. Nobody can know that from the visible half, so the best a pixel model can do is average over all plausible grass, and its error budget is eaten by texture.

Balestriero and LeCun made this precise in 2024: learning by reconstruction pushes features toward the high-variance subspaces of the input, and for natural images those are mostly low-level texture and lighting, which carry little of what perception needs. The fix in a JEPA is to move the target: encode the hidden part with a network, and predict that encoding. The encoder decides what is worth predicting.

≈
Picture it

A pixel model is a court sketch artist asked to draw the suspect's face from a witness who saw only the shoes. A JEPA is a detective asked to say "tall, left-handed, in a hurry". Both are predictions. Only one is possible.

Beat 2

Why does I-JEPA hide big blocks instead of scattered patches?

Because a scattered patch can be guessed by copying its neighbours, which teaches nothing. A big block is far from anything visible, so the only way to predict it is to understand the scene.

I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) cuts the image into 14 × 14 patches, shows the context encoder one large block, and asks the predictor for the embeddings of four separate target blocks. The paper found that this multi-block recipe, with targets large enough to be "semantic", mattered more than almost any other choice. The lab below shows why with the dumbest possible model: one that fills each hidden patch with the colour of the closest visible patch. When that cheat works well, the task is too easy to teach anything.

Beat 3

Why does a JEPA need three networks?

Because the thing you predict (the target embedding) is itself learned. If the same network both made the target and chased it with full gradients, the easiest solution would be to make every embedding identical. The third network, a slow copy, keeps the target honest.

Beat 4

Why does a pixel predictor see ghosts when the future forks?

Because the squared-error-optimal prediction is the average of all possible futures, and the average of "ball goes left" and "ball goes right" is two half-transparent balls. A JEPA can drop the unknowable bit from its embedding, or carry it in a latent variable z.

Beat 5

Why is a prediction error useful even after training?

Because a model that predicts well is calm when the world behaves and spikes when it doesn't. That spike is "surprise", and it detects impossible physics, anomalies and moments worth waking a bigger model for, with no labels.

Developmental psychologists test babies with violation of expectation: show a ball rolling behind a screen, then make it reappear somewhere it could not be. Babies stare longer. Garrido et al. (2025) ran the same test on V-JEPA: its latent prediction error jumps on impossible videos, and it beat pixel-generative models and multimodal LLMs, which sat near chance.

The same signal is an edge feature. An anomaly detector built on JEPA surprise needs no decoder (see MTS-JEPA), and VL-JEPA uses embedding change to decide when to call its text decoder, about 2.85 times less often at equal quality.

CarefulSurprise shows the model has an expectation. It does not show the model "understands physics" the way we do. A model can be surprised for the wrong reasons, for instance by a lighting change it never saw.
Beat 6

Why are there so many JEPAs?

Because the recipe only needs two things: a way to hide part of the input, and an encoder for that kind of input. Anything you can cut into pieces (frames, notes, atoms, proof steps) gets its own JEPA.

Three things to remember

  1. Predict the summary, not the picture.A pixel loss pays for every unpredictable speck. An embedding target lets the encoder decide what is worth predicting.
  2. Hide what you can't copy.Big target blocks far from the context remove the neighbour-copy shortcut, so the only path to low loss is understanding.
  3. Averages of futures are ghosts.When the future forks, pixel MSE predicts a blur. A JEPA drops the unknowable bit, or carries it in z.
