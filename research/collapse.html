Part 1 · Predict · Chapter 03

Why doesn't everything collapse to one point?

something in the training rule makes it cost more to forget than to remember. A loss that only asks two views to agree is solved perfectly by an encoder that outputs the same vector for everything. Every working method adds a counter-force: a target that cannot move (stop-gradient and a slow copy), a penalty on shrinking variance (VICReg), or a test that the embeddings look like an isotropic Gaussian (SIGReg). Which directions survive is a contest between that force and decay, and for a linear JEPA it can be computed.

Beat 1

Why is collapse the easy way out?

Because "make the two embeddings agree" has a zero-cost answer: ignore the input. Gradient descent finds it quickly, since shrinking every embedding toward one point lowers the disagreement on every example at once.

Reconstruction cannot collapse: its target is the raw data, which the model does not control. Joint-embedding methods (BYOL, VICReg, every JEPA) chose a learned target to escape the pixel trap of chapter 01, and that freedom opens a trap of its own. If both sides of the loss are produced by networks, both can move toward the same constant.

Collapse comes in two strengths. Complete collapse is the extreme: every input maps to one point. Dimensional collapse (Jing et al., 2022) is quieter and more common in practice: the embeddings vary, but only along a few directions, so a 256-dimensional embedding might really be 10-dimensional. The standard gauge is the effective rank.

erank(Z) = exp( −Σk qk log qk ), qk = σk / Σj σjσk are the singular values of the centred embedding matrix. In 2-D: 2 means a full cloud, 1 a line, and a single point has no spread at all.

Beat 2

Why do a stop-gradient, a slow copy and a predictor prevent it?

Because with a stop-gradient the target is frozen for each step, so the online network can only move toward it and cannot drag it down to meet it. The slow EMA copy keeps the target from following too fast, and the predictor gives the dynamics a stable non-collapsed solution. Tian, Chen and Ganguli (2021) showed why in linear networks.

BYOL (Grill et al., 2020) surprised the field: no negative pairs, no variance penalty, and still no collapse. It used an online network with a predictor head, a target network updated as an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online one, and a stop-gradient on the target. SimSiam (Chen and He, 2020) then removed the EMA and kept working, but collapsed the moment the stop-gradient was removed. The lab above reproduces that last fact: "Predictor, no stop-grad" goes to a point.

Tian, Chen and Ganguli analysed the linear case. The loss alone has collapse as a global minimum, but these systems never reach it, because of the dynamics. With stop-gradient the encoder and predictor evolve so that the predictor lines up with the eigenbasis of the encoder's output correlation, after which each eigen-direction grows or shrinks on its own. They tested this by skipping the trained predictor entirely: DirectPred sets it from the eigen-decomposition of the correlation matrix and worked about as well as a trained one.

That eigen-direction picture is the seed of everything below. Each direction of the data has its own fate. Which ones survive? Beat 4 answers that exactly for a linear JEPA.

without stop-grad

Both sides chase each other

Gradient flows into the target too. The fastest way to make two outputs equal is to shrink both: ‖f(x₁) − f(x₂)‖² → 0 with f ≡ c.

with stop-grad + EMA

The target holds still

Only the online side moves. The target is a slow average of past online weights, θ̄ ← τθ̄ + (1−τ)θ, so a collapse has to happen in the online net first and is not rewarded along the way.

with a predictor

An extra degree of freedom

The predictor can absorb the mismatch between views per direction instead of the encoder throwing directions away.

Beat 3

Why does VICReg need three terms?

Because each term blocks one failure. Invariance makes the views agree. Variance stops complete collapse by keeping every dimension's spread above a floor. Covariance stops dimensional collapse by making dimensions carry different information. Drop one and the matching failure returns.

VICReg (Bardes, Ponce and LeCun, 2022) replaces the stop-gradient trick with explicit statistics of the batch. It needs no EMA, no predictor and no negative pairs, and its objective says in plain terms what "not collapsed" means.

L = λ·s(Z, Z′) + μ·[ v(Z) + v(Z′) ] + ν·[ c(Z) + c(Z′) ]Z and Z′ are a batch of embeddings of two views. The paper's defaults are λ = μ = 25 and ν = 1.
Invariance smean ‖zᵢ − z′ᵢ‖²Two views of one input should embed together.Alone: collapses to a point.
Variance v(1/d) Σⱼ max(0, γ − √(Var zⱼ + ε))Each dimension keeps a standard deviation of at least γ = 1 across the batch.Blocks: complete collapse.
Covariance c(1/d) Σi≠j C(Z)ᵢⱼ²Off-diagonal covariances are pushed to zero, so dimensions stop copying each other.Blocks: dimensional collapse.

The sim in beat 1 shows each term doing its job. With variance alone both outputs pick the same clean direction, because the invariance term prefers it and nothing forbids two dimensions from being the same. The covariance term is the only thing that says "be different".

C-JEPA (Mo and Tong, NeurIPS 2024) brought this back into JEPAs. They argued that I-JEPA's EMA does not fully prevent collapse and that its mean patch embeddings are poorly estimated, and added VICReg's variance and covariance terms to the I-JEPA objective, reporting faster and more stable pretraining. The two families from chapter 01 meet here: alignment's regularizers protect latent prediction.

Picture it

A choir told only "sing the same note as your neighbour" will happily all hum one note, or fall silent. Variance says "everyone must be audible". Covariance says "and the sopranos may not just double the altos". Only then do you get chords.

Beat 4

Which directions escape collapse, and why those?

Because near the collapsed point each data direction feels two forces: a drive from predicting the target, and a decay from the predictor's own penalty on the masked context. A direction escapes when drive beats decay (μ > 1). Masking and predictor scale move μ; the EMA rate does not.

De Melo Costa et al. (2026, Drive vs. Decay) linearize JEPA training at the collapsed point. Each mode obeys one small equation, and the ratio μ = drive/decay decides its fate. The count of unstable modes bounds the effective rank, and they validated the rule on more than 800 Tabular-JEPA configurations.

λ² + (β + σ)·λ + β·σ·(1 − μ) = 0, μ = γ / σPer mode: σ is the decay (from the masked-context covariance and WₚᵀWₚ), γ the drive (from the context-target cross-covariance and the target mask), β the EMA rate. A positive root exists iff μ > 1. β multiplies everything, so it sets speed, not sign.

The project's first notebook derived μ in closed form for stationary data (any signal whose covariance has a constant diagonal d̄, such as image or audio patches), with a fixed predictor Wₚ = αI, context keep probability p and target keep probability ptgt:

μk = (ptgt / α) · λk / ( p·λk + (1 − p)·d̄ )
escapes iff λk > (1 − p)·d̄ / (ptgt/α − p)λk is the data variance along mode k. The second factor is above 1 exactly for modes with more than average variance: masking is a spectral filter.
This project · notebook 1The closed form is the project's own derivation, not the paper's. Checked against plain minibatch SGD with sampled masks: predicted and measured early growth rates agree within 0.008 across all 32 modes, and the escaping-mode count matches at 14 of 16 settings (the two misses are modes within about 2% of μ = 1). Later in training, minibatch noise leaks growth into every mode, but effective rank stayed at 2.05, under the bound of 5, matching the paper's caveat that the bound covers the growing component only. Not yet tested: a trained predictor, nonlinear encoders, non-stationary data. See jepa/notebooks/01-drive-vs-decay.ipynb.

The predictor-scale result is the paper's argument for ResidualPred, an identity-biased predictor that keeps α near 1. It also explains a fact from chapter 01 from the inside: this linear JEPA learns only the high-variance end of the spectrum, so its embedding is anisotropic by construction. The next beat is about a regularizer that pushes the other way.

Beat 5

Why test random 1-D shadows instead of the whole distribution?

Because a distribution is fixed by all of its one-dimensional projections (Cramér–Wold), and a 1-D test against a Gaussian is cheap and reliable while a high-dimensional one is not. LeJEPA's SIGReg samples random directions, tests each shadow, and averages, which removes the need for stop-gradient and EMA.

LeJEPA (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025) starts from a target instead of a trick. They prove that an isotropic Gaussian embedding minimizes the worst-case risk of downstream linear probes, then enforce it with SIGReg: draw M random unit directions, project the batch onto each, and measure how far each projection is from N(0, 1) with a characteristic-function test. One trade-off hyperparameter replaces the EMA schedule, the stop-gradient and the predictor tricks. Why this is the right target is chapter 05; this beat is about why the test works.

VICReg checks only second moments: the covariance. A cloud can have identity covariance and still be badly non-Gaussian, for example two blobs. The characteristic function φ(t) = E[eitx] sees all moments at once, and comparing it with e−t²/2 catches both the wrong scale and the wrong shape.

T(u) = N · ∫ | φ̂u(t) − e−t²/2 |²
· e−t²/2 dt
φ̂u(t) = (1/N) Σn ei t ⟨u, zn⟩An Epps–Pulley-style statistic for one direction u. SIGReg averages it over random directions; the lab computes it on a grid of t values.
Beat 6

Why pick one cure over another?

Because they leave different geometry behind. EMA and stop-gradient prevent collapse but promise nothing about the shape of what survives. VICReg fixes the covariance. SIGReg aims at the whole distribution, and in this project's pilot it was the one that survived quantization best.

Three things to remember

  1. Agreement alone is free.Any loss that only asks two learned outputs to match is solved by a constant. Something must make forgetting cost more than remembering.
  2. Variance gives a line; covariance gives a plane.Each VICReg term blocks one kind of collapse. Without decorrelation, dimensions copy the easiest direction.
  3. Drive beats decay, mode by mode.A direction escapes iff μ > 1. Masking filters by variance, a large predictor kills modes, and the EMA rate only sets the speed.
