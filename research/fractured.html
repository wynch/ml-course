Part 2 · Theory · Chapter 07

Why can the same answer hide a broken inside?

training only scores the output, and many different internal programs produce the same output. The path decides which program you get. Kumar, Clune, Lehman and Stanley (2025) show an image network found by open-ended human search that computes symmetry once and reuses it, and an SGD-trained network with the same architecture and the same image that is a patchwork inside. Any test that looks only at outputs, including the similarity scores behind the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, cannot tell the two apart.

Beat 1

Why can two networks with identical outputs be different inside?

Because a loss function only sees what comes out. Two programs that print the same picture can be organised completely differently, and gradient descent has no reason to prefer the tidy one when the messy one is closer.

The paper is a position paper from May 2025: Questioning Representational Optimism in Deep Learning: The Fractured Entangled Representation Hypothesis, by Akarsh Kumar (MIT, Isola's lab), Jeff Clune, Joel Lehman and Kenneth Stanley. Its target is representational optimism, the unstated belief that good internal representations come for free as models scale and their scores improve. It names two ends of a spectrum:

The bad case

FER · fractured entangled representation

Fractured: information that should form one concept is split into disconnected, redundant pieces. Entangled: behaviours that should be independent influence each other "in idiosyncratic and inappropriate ways".

The good case

UFR · unified factored representation

Unified: "a single unbroken function for each key capability". Factored: "keeping independent capabilities separated so that they do not interfere with each other". The authors grant that perfect UFR may be out of reach.

≈
Picture it

Two programs pass the same test suite. One defines mirror() once and calls it everywhere. The other copy-pastes a slightly different mirror into every function, and fixing a bug in one copy breaks two others. The tests are green for both. You only find out which one you have when you try to add a feature.

The paper calls a network with the right outputs and FER insides an imposter, and on MLST (2025-07) Kumar and Stanley extend the word to frontier LLMs. Stanley's one-line version: "it matters not just where you get, but how you got there."

Beat 2

Why does a network found by open-ended search reuse its regularities?

Because each step of the search had to produce something a person liked, with no target in mind. Symmetry was liked early, long before any skull, so it was built once and every later feature was added on top of it. SGD aims at the finished image directly and never needs a reusable piece along the way.

Picbreeder (around 2007) was a website where people bred images. Each image is drawn by a CPPN: a small network that takes a pixel's coordinates (x, y, distance from centre d) and outputs its colour. NEAT mutates the network (nudges weights, adds nodes and connections). A user picks a favourite out of 15 children, which becomes the next parent. Nobody is optimising toward a target.

The FER experiment takes the famous Skull, reached in about 74 generations. Symmetry appeared around generations 20 to 36, long before anything looked like a skull. The authors convert the NEAT graph into an equivalent dense MLP ("layerization"), then train the same MLP from random weights with SGD to output the same skull, in about 10,000 steps. Both outputs match. Then they plot every neuron's activation over the image plane:

  • The Picbreeder network encodes left-right symmetry early and reuses it; its layerized form has only 24 novel intermediate representations.
  • The SGD network becomes symmetric only at the last layer; inside it is a "highly entangled patchwork". Looking in a PCA basis or along random directions did not rescue it.

The lab rebuilds this in miniature. The left network is a NEAT-style CPPN you can breed; it starts from a hand-built seed in the spirit of Picbreeder genomes (it is not the paper's skull). The right network is a dense 3 × 8 MLP over (x, y, d), trained live by backprop to reproduce whatever the left one draws.

Beat 3

Why does nudging a single weight reveal how a network is organised?

Because in a factored network one weight controls one meaningful thing, so changing it changes that thing and leaves the rest alone. In a fractured network every concept is spread over many weights and every weight touches many concepts, so a nudge smears the whole image.

The FER paper sweeps single weights and watches the output. In the Picbreeder skull, weights act as semantic knobs: one opens and closes the mouth, one makes an eye wink, one widens the jaw. In the SGD skull, the same sweeps give "meaningless distortions". The method goes back to Huizinga, Stanley and Clune's work on canalization in Picbreeder: lineages shaped by open-ended selection make mutations land on meaningful dimensions.

Below, pick a network and a weight, then drag. The sweep strip shows the image at seven settings. The two statistics on the right are computed over every weight in each network: how often a nudge of ±1 keeps the face left-right symmetric, and how much of the image an average nudge touches.

Beat 4

Why suspect the same thing inside language models, and why is the evidence weak?

Because LLMs show the behavioural signature of fracture: a skill works in one wording and fails in a near-identical one, as if it were stored in pieces. But behaviour is indirect evidence, the image experiment has tens to hundreds of connections, and SGD-trained sequence models have been shown to factor cleanly on synthetic data.

The paper's claimed costs are where data is thin: generalization at the frontier, creativity (inventing means manipulating the true regularities of a thing, like the iPhone recombining phone and computer), and continual learning, because new learning "does not propagate to related cases" when the concept lives in many copies.

LimitsStanley himself (Jim Rutt Show, 2025-08) concedes the experiments are tiny, the LLM evidence is indirect, and there is no standard metric for representation quality. Shai et al. (2026-02) show transformers trained by next-token prediction put conditionally independent world factors into orthogonal subspaces, prefer factoring early even when it is lossy, and see the same in RNNs and LSTMs. They say outright that this "brings reason for representational optimism". No formal replication or rebuttal of the skull experiment was found, and the citation graph could not be pulled, so smaller follow-ups may be missing.
Beat 5

Why do different models still seem to converge on the same picture of the world?

Because they are all fit to the same world, and many tasks plus big capacity plus a simplicity bias squeeze them toward its structure. By 2026 the claim is narrower: models agree on which things are near each other, locally, much more than on exact distances, and that agreement says nothing about how the inside is organised.

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis (Huh, Cheung, Wang, Isola, ICML 2024) says representations across architectures, objectives and even modalities are converging toward a shared statistical model of reality. The measure is mutual k-nearest-neighbour alignment: embed the same N items with two models, find each item's k nearest neighbours in each space, and average the overlap.

mKNN(A, B) = (1/N) Σi |kNNA(i) ∩ kNNB(i)| / k chance ≈ k / (N − 1)It compares neighbourhoods, not coordinates, so it works across models with different dimensions. Stronger language models score higher against vision models.

Support in 2025: vec2vec translates between text-embedding spaces with no paired data; text-only LLMs align more with vision models when prompted to "imagine seeing"; DINOv3 and Web-SSL reach CLIP-level semantics without language. Then three 2026 papers narrowed it. The Aristotelian view (Gröger, Wen, Brbić, ICML 2026) calibrates the metrics against width and depth with permutation nulls: global convergence largely vanishes, local neighbourhood agreement survives. Back into Plato's Cave (Koepke, Zverev, Ginosar, Efros) finds mKNN alignment collapses as the gallery grows and is inflated by one-to-one image-caption data; modalities share coarse categories, "overlapping Umwelten". Structure over Geometry (You, Jang, Mo, Jung, 2026-09) finds which samples are related converges, metric distances much less.

So are PRH and FER enemies? They look opposed (FER cites PRH as an example of optimism) but measure different things. PRH reads the kernel: who is near whom in the final embedding. FER is about how the computation is organised: whether a concept is computed once and whether separate concepts can be changed separately. PRH's evidence comes from dense data; FER worries about the sparse frontier. The 2026 critiques, which find only local and relational agreement, leave exactly the room underneath that FER predicts. Kumar's November 2025 talk title, "Towards a Platonic Intelligence with Unified Factored Representations", reads them as complementary: PRH says where representations head, UFR says what quality a truly good one should have (that reading comes from the title only). Isola is Kumar's advisor; this is one lab's two questions, not two camps.

Beat 6

Why would the order of learning shape what is learned?

Because whatever is learned first becomes a building block, and later learning is cheapest when it reuses existing blocks. Learn symmetry first and every feature is built on it. Go straight for the finished target and nothing is ever forced to become a shared piece.

The FER paper puts it bluntly: "the order in which a concept is learned is fundamental to the representation eventually achieved". It is the stepping stones idea from Stanley and Lehman's Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned (2015) moved inside a network. The paper floats mitigations without testing them: grokking-style cleanup, modified SGD, mixture-of-experts or pruning, and open-ended search (novelty search, MAP-Elites, POET), for which nobody knows a way to pair with SGD at scale.

Outside FER, path dependence is now well documented for small systems and for fine-tuning, and suggestive for pretraining:

  • Structure first. Kumar and colleagues' 2026 NCA pre-pre-training: 164M tokens of neural-cellular-automaton dynamics before language beat 1.6B tokens of real C4 text, with the transferable benefit carried by attention layers. The best NCA complexity depended on the target domain.
  • Procedures first. Laura Ruis (MLST 2025-01): LLM maths reasoning draws on documents that demonstrate procedures, spread across many sources, while facts come from specific documents.
  • Update rule matters. Lampinen et al. (2025): from the same data, in-context learning generalizes more flexibly than fine-tuning (reversals, syllogisms). RL's Razor (MIT 2025): on-policy RL forgets less than supervised fine-tuning, because it finds solutions closest in KL to what the model already does.
  • Late cleanup. Grokking (chapter 06): weight decay replaces memorization with a compact algorithm long after training accuracy saturates.

Kumar's papers, read as one programme ("can we get the benefits of open-ended, evolution-like processes inside deep learning?"):

Beat 7

What does a fractured inside change for JEPA, interpretability and open-endedness?

It adds a question every field had skipped: not just "does it predict well" or "does it agree with other models", but "is each capability computed once, and can it be changed on its own?" Each field has a ready-made tool to ask it.

JEPA

Isotropic is not factored

LeJEPA's SIGReg pushes embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian, a global property of the output distribution (chapter 05). It says nothing about modular reuse inside the encoder. FER's weight sweeps could test whether JEPA encoders are more UFR-like than pixel-reconstruction or contrastive ones. No paper has done it.

Interpretability

Feature splitting looks like fracture

Sparse autoencoders try to recover factored directions from entangled neurons. A concept split across many SAE latents (feature splitting, absorption) is FER-like fracture seen from the inside (chapter 08). Shai et al.'s orthogonal factor subspaces are interpretability evidence against universal FER.

Open-endedness

Why the path is worth searching

FER is the argument that open-ended search matters for representations, not only for finding artifacts (chapter 14). Neural Thickets (Gan & Isola, ICML 2026) adds a twist: random perturbations of large pretrained models are often already task experts, so evolution-style search on top of SGD pretraining is viable.

(2) Replicate the skull from akarshkumar0101/fer, train the SGD version far longer under weight decay, and check whether symmetry moves into early layers. Minutes per run on CPU.

Three things to remember

  1. Same output, different program.A loss sees only what comes out. Two networks can draw the same skull, one with symmetry computed once and reused, one as a patchwork.
  2. Nudge a weight to see the inside.In a unified, factored network one knob moves one part. In a fractured one every nudge smears everything.
  3. Agreeing on neighbours is not agreeing on how.Models converge on which things are related, locally. That says nothing about whether their insides are organised well.
