Beat 1

Why doesn't a JEPA cost more on the device?

Because two of its three networks exist only for training. The predictor and the slow EMA copy are thrown away, and what ships is one encoder plus a small head. A JEPA ViT-Tiny costs exactly what any ViT-Tiny costs.

The objective changes what the encoder knows, not how many multiplications it takes. A ViT-Tiny has about 5.5M parameters (192-wide, 12 blocks, 3 heads) whether it was trained by I-JEPA, by MAE or with labels. So "JEPA is good for the edge" cannot mean "the JEPA encoder is cheaper". It means two other things: a JEPA can make a small encoder better, because it does not spend capacity on unpredictable pixel detail (chapter 04), and it changes what you do downstream, where you can stay in latent space instead of going back to pixels or tokens.

Where the edge story gets interesting is the column "kept in a world model". LeWorldModel (LeWM, 2026) is about 15M parameters in total: a ViT-Tiny encoder of about 5M and a predictor of about 10M. It trains in a few hours on one L40S and encodes each frame as a single 192-dimensional vector. That last detail is where the next beat starts.

Beat 2

Why is planning in latent space so much cheaper?

Because a planner calls its predictor tens of thousands of times per decision, and every call costs in proportion to the size of the state. Shrink each imagined frame from a grid of about 200 patch tokens to one 192-d vector and every one of those calls gets cheaper.

A latent world model plans by imagination. The usual recipe is the cross-entropy method (CEM) inside model-predictive control: sample N action sequences, roll each out for H steps with the predictor, score them by distance to the goal embedding, refit, repeat I times, execute the first action, replan. So the bill for one decision is

cost per decision ≈ N × I × H × (predictor cost per step)The first three factors are the search. The last factor is the state: a transformer step on n tokens costs about 2·P·n FLOPs for P parameters, plus attention, which grows like n². Only the last factor depends on how you encode a frame.

DINO-WM (2024) plans on frozen DINOv2 patch features, a grid of patch tokens per frame. LeWM plans on one token per frame, about 200 times fewer. LeWM reports planning in 0.98 s versus 47 s for DINO-WM, about 48× faster. V-JEPA 2-AC reported about 16 s per action versus about 4 minutes for the pixel-generative Cosmos baseline, which has to render video to imagine. A controller that never draws a pixel is the kind of thing that fits on a robot's onboard computer.

Beat 3

Why call the expensive decoder only when the picture changes?

Because consecutive frames of a scene have almost the same embedding, so decoding each one repeats the same sentence. A JEPA gives you a cheap continuous stream of embeddings, and a change score on that stream says when something new is there to describe.

VL-JEPA (2025) predicts the embedding of a text answer instead of generating tokens, and calls its text decoder only when that embedding shifts. On EgoExo4D, decoding at 0.35 Hz matched uniform 1 Hz decoding, about 2.85× fewer decoder calls at equal quality. That is an always-on camera design: a small encoder runs on the device all the time, and the expensive model (a captioner, a VLM, an upload) is woken by events. The same signal is a built-in anomaly score: prediction error in latent space needs no decoder, unlike autoencoder-based detection.

The sim below is a stream of 32-d embeddings. Each scene has its own centre; inside a scene the embedding drifts slowly and jitters with noise; scenes change at random moments (red marks), and about 4 in 10 changes are subtle: the new scene is mostly the old one. The policy is one line: decode when the cosine distance between the current embedding and the one at the last decode exceeds a threshold.

Beat 4

Why is JEPA training cheap, and when is it not?

Because there is no decoder, the target is a small embedding, and capacity is not spent on unpredictable detail, so it converges in fewer steps. The catch is memory: anything that backpropagates through a second full view pays for it in activations.

I-JEPA was pitched as efficient from the start. A ViT-H/14 trained on ImageNet in under 72 hours on 16 A100s (under 1,200 GPU-hours), more than 10× cheaper than MAE ViT-H/14. Each iteration was about 7% slower than MAE; the saving came from converging in about 5× fewer iterations. The reasons given: one view per image, no augmentations, a context encoder that sees about 25% of the patches, and embedding targets. Fewer steps to useful features is exactly what Balestriero and LeCun's result predicts: reconstruction allocates capacity to high-variance directions, a JEPA learns features in order of predictiveness (Littwin et al., 2024).

Beat 5

Why would a round cloud of embeddings survive fewer bits?

Because quantization lays one evenly spaced ruler over all the numbers, and the ruler's length is set by the most extreme value. A round, light-tailed (isotropic Gaussian) cloud uses the ruler well. One spiky outlier channel stretches the ruler so far that every other number falls between the same two marks.

Post-training quantization stores each number as a small integer times a scale. The symmetric round-to-nearest version used in this project's experiments is

q(x) = s · clamp(round(x / s), −Q, Q), Q = 2b−1 − 1, s = max|x| / QWith b = 4 bits there are 15 levels (−7…7); with 3 bits, 7; with 2 bits, only 3 (−1, 0, 1). "Per-tensor" uses one s for every channel; "per-channel" gives each channel its own s. The rounding error per number is at most s/2.

Every term in that formula points at the same weakness: s is set by the maximum. Heavy tails (high kurtosis) and outlier channels make the maximum large compared with the typical value, so most numbers land on a handful of levels. A strongly anisotropic cloud (high condition number) has thin directions; the rounding noise is the same size in every direction, so it can swamp a thin direction that carries information.

Now the theory. LeJEPA's SIGReg pushes embeddings toward an isotropic Gaussian, and a 2026 free-energy analysis of SIGReg predicts a condition number near 1. A Gaussian also has zero excess kurtosis, so no fat tails. Put the two facts together and you get a hypothesis nobody had tested: SIGReg-trained encoders should tolerate low-bit quantization better than VICReg- or EMA-trained ones. The lab lets you see the mechanism before the evidence.

▤
Picture it

You are measuring a class of children with a tape that has only seven marks, and the tape must reach the tallest person in the room. If one visitor is a giraffe, the marks are a metre apart and every child is "about one mark". Per-channel scaling gives the giraffe its own tape. SIGReg's bet is simpler: train so that nobody in the room is a giraffe.

Beat 6

What did this project's pilot find, and why is nobody else checking?

Because no JEPA-specific quantization, pruning or low-bit paper exists yet; the closest, a 2026 tutorial on JEPA for 6G networks, lists quantization as future work with no results. Our CPU pilot found SIGReg lost the least accuracy in every quantized setting, and that kurtosis predicted the loss better than condition number.

Notebook 3 is deliberately tiny so it runs anywhere in about 4 minutes: scikit-learn's 8 × 8 digits, a 3-layer MLP encoder with a 16-d embedding, half the pixels hidden from the context, and three anti-collapse schemes that are otherwise identical (SIGReg LeJEPA-style, VICReg, and an EMA teacher with stop-gradient), 4,000 Adam steps, 3 seeds each. A linear probe is fitted on full-precision embeddings and kept fixed, as it would be in deployment. Then either the weights (per output channel) or the output embedding (per tensor) are quantized.

Read the three bar groups side by side. If condition number drove the damage, VICReg (4,275) would be the disaster. It isn't: its channels have nearly equal scales and light tails, so embedding quantization hurt it only a little more than SIGReg. EMA's condition number is moderate (21), but its tails are heavy (kurtosis about 5), and it fell apart at INT4 and INT3. Tail heaviness tracked embedding-quantization loss better than condition number, which is what the lab above shows too: the ruler is set by the maximum. INT8 cost nothing for any method.

Why this is only a pilotToy data and model, 3 seeds, untuned hyperparameters, and EMA starts from a lower full-precision accuracy, which confounds its drop. Round-to-nearest only: methods like SmoothQuant or GPTQ exist precisely to repair outliers like EMA's. VICReg's collapse under 3-bit weights (37.1 points) is unexplained; its weights may carry outliers its embedding does not show.
Running nowThe real test is ViT-Tiny (5.5M parameters) on CIFAR-100 with the same three regularizers, measuring W8/W4/W3 and E8/E4/E3 plus kurtosis, condition number, effective rank and residual-stream outlier ratios. The first seed is training overnight on the M5; results are not in yet. What would confirm or refute the hypothesis is written down before the numbers arrive, in chapter 17.

Three things to remember

  1. The encoder is ordinary; the pipeline is not.A JEPA encoder costs what its shape costs. The savings come from staying in latent space: planning without pixels, decoding only on change.
  2. Every imagined step pays for the state.Planning cost is samples × iterations × horizon × predictor cost, and only the last factor knows how big a frame is. One token instead of 200 is the whole trick.
  3. The ruler is set by the giant.Low-bit quantization fails on tails and outliers. An isotropic Gaussian has neither, which is why SIGReg might be a free recipe for edge models. Kurtosis is the number to watch.
