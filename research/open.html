Why can't greatness be planned?

the stepping stones to an ambitious goal rarely look like progress toward it. A search that rewards "closer to the goal" climbs into the nearest dead end. A search that rewards "different from what we have" collects stepping stones, and some of them lead where no measure of closeness pointed.

Beat 1

Why does aiming straight at the goal keep you from reaching it?

Because a fitness function scores where you are, not where a step can lead. When the route to the goal first goes away from it, every step along that route scores worse than the dead end next to the goal, so selection throws the route away. That is a deceptive objective.

Joel Lehman and Kenneth Stanley tested the idea with the simplest deceptive problem they could build: a robot in a maze, scored by how close it ends to the goal (ALIFE 2008, then "Abandoning Objectives", Evolutionary Computation 2011). Then they did something that sounds absurd. They threw the goal away and rewarded novelty alone: how far a robot's final position is from the final positions already seen. Novelty search solved the maze far more often, because "go somewhere new" keeps pushing into corridors that "get closer" refuses to enter.

novelty(x) = (1/k) · Σi=1..k dist( b(x), b(μi) ) b(x) is a behaviour descriptor (here: where the robot ends). μ1..k are the k nearest behaviours among the current population and an archive of past novel ones. No goal appears anywhere.

The lab is a re-creation, not the original. Each robot is a list of 24 headings; it moves 24 short segments and slides along walls. Both populations use the same mutation, the same population size (100) and the same selection (keep the top 10, mutate them). Only the score differs.

Picture it

A hiker told "always walk uphill toward the summit you can see" ends on the nearest false peak. A hiker told "always go somewhere you have not been" eventually stands on every peak, including the real one, though nobody told them which it was.

Beat 2

Why keep a thousand mediocre solutions instead of one good one?

Because each one is the best-known way to do something slightly different, and any of them may be the stepping stone to the solution you need later. Quality-diversity search keeps the best solution per kind of behaviour, so it gets diversity and quality at once.

MAP-Elites (Jean-Baptiste Mouret and Jeff Clune, 2015) is the cleanest version. Pick a few behaviour dimensions and cut them into a grid. Keep one elite per cell, the best solution whose behaviour falls in that cell. Then loop: pick a random elite, mutate it, evaluate it, and put the child in its own cell if the cell is empty or the child beats the incumbent. Cully, Clune, Tarapore and Mouret used such a map of walking gaits to let a six-legged robot recover from a broken leg by trying elites from the map (Nature, 2015).

The lab is the standard toy from the quality-diversity literature: a planar arm with 8 joints. The behaviour is where the hand ends up (a 2-D grid); the quality is smoothness (all joint angles alike, so low variance is better). Each cell of the map is literally the place the hand reaches, so you can click a cell and see the arm.

Beat 3

Why does the path to a great result go through things that look unrelated?

Because the stepping stones are judged by what they make possible, and that is invisible to a similarity score. In Picbreeder, the images that led to a skull did not look like skulls; they built symmetry first. Searching for the skull directly scores those ancestors as failures.

Picbreeder (Secretan, Stanley and colleagues, 2008) was a website where people bred images. Each image is drawn by a CPPN. A user picks the images they like from 15 children, those get mutated, and anyone can continue from anyone else's image. Nobody had a target. Users still produced skulls, butterflies, cars and faces.

Stanley noticed that when someone found a car, they had not been looking for a car. And when people tried to evolve a specific image as a target, they failed. In the FER paper (chapter 07) the famous skull took about 74 picks along a path where left-right symmetry appeared around picks 20 to 36, long before anything looked like a skull. The resulting network uses symmetry once and reuses it; an SGD-trained network that draws the same skull is a tangle. In Why Greatness Cannot Be Planned (2015), Stanley and Lehman make the same point about history: the people who built vacuum tubes were not trying to build computers.

How the lab was madeWe ran a novelty search over small fixed-topology CPPNs (8 + 4 hidden units, activations sin, Gaussian, sigmoid, abs, tanh, identity; Picbreeder's NEAT also grows the topology, which we skip). From its archive we took one image, an "eye", and recorded its 24-step lineage from the first random image. The lab replays that lineage, scores every ancestor by pixel correlation with the eye, and lets you run a greedy search that tries to evolve the eye directly from the same first image.
Beat 4

Why let the problems evolve too, and who decides which are interesting?

Because a fixed problem runs out of stepping stones: once it is solved, nothing new is asked. Co-evolving environments keep generating new problems at the edge of what the agents can do, and a model of human interest keeps that stream from filling with trivial variations.

POET (Wang, Lehman, Clune and Stanley, 2019; GECCO best paper) evolves pairs of 2-D walking terrains and walkers. New terrains are kept only if they are neither too easy nor too hard for current walkers, and walkers are regularly transferred between terrains. Some hard terrains were solved only by a walker that had first learned on a different terrain, a stepping stone no curriculum designer would have picked. Enhanced POET (2020) added a general measure of novelty for environments and an unbounded terrain encoding.

The weak point was "interesting". An algorithm can generate endless levels that differ only in irrelevant ways. OMNI (Zhang, Lehman, Stanley and Clune, 2023) asks a language model which tasks a human would find interesting and trains on tasks that are both learnable and interesting. OMNI-EPIC (2024) lets foundation models write new environments and their reward functions in code, filtered the same way, for a potentially endless stream of tasks.

Jeff Clune's AI-generating algorithms (2019) names the program: meta-learn the architectures, meta-learn the learning algorithms, and generate the learning environments. Clune on MLST (2025-01): "Agent AI needs Darwin".

Beat 5

What exactly makes a process open-ended?

A system is open-ended for an observer when its outputs keep surprising the observer (novelty) and the observer can still learn to predict them from enough history (learnability). Noise fails the second test, a repeating pattern fails the first.

Edward Hughes, Michael Dennis, Jack Parker-Holder, Tim Rocktäschel and colleagues at Google DeepMind turned the slogan into a definition in "Open-Endedness is Essential for Artificial Superhuman Intelligence" (ICML 2024). Take a system producing artifacts over time and an observer with a model that predicts them.

novel: the observer's loss on artifacts from later and later times keeps rising
learnable: at any time, giving the observer a longer history lowers its loss Both are measured relative to the observer. The same stream can be open-ended for a mouse and boring for a physicist, or the reverse.

Rocktäschel's MLST argument (2024-10) is that foundation models finally make this practical: they are good observers (models of what humans find interesting) and good generators (of environments, prompts and programs). Promptbreeder, Rainbow Teaming and Genie world models are his examples.

Why a JEPA fits this definitionThe definition is written in terms of an observer's prediction loss. A JEPA is an observer whose whole job is prediction in a learned latent space: surprise is its prediction error, and learnability is how fast that error falls with more context. That is the project's bridge idea from chapter 13: a JEPA encoder as the judge in ASAL, or as the behaviour space for novelty search, so that "new" means "my world model did not expect this" rather than "these pixels differ".
Still openNo accepted metric of open-endedness exists. Lehman and Stanley's Knightian Blindspot paper (2025) argues ML's formalisms (fixed environments, expected-value objectives) leave out unknown unknowns, which evolution survives by persisting, diversifying and building niches. Edward Hughes (MLST 2026-09): creativity is not optimisation; the missing piece is judgement about which questions are worth asking.
Beat 6

Why are AI scientists and self-improving agents built as archives?

Because the same deception hits them. An agent that only keeps its single best version gets stuck on a local peak; keeping an archive of many working variants, and branching from old ones, lets a temporarily worse change become the stepping stone to a much better one. Where a cheap verifier exists, the archive plus the verifier does the rest.

Large language models changed the mutation operator. ELM (Lehman et al., 2022) used an LLM to propose code mutations. FunSearch and then AlphaEvolve (Novikov, Balog and colleagues at Google DeepMind, 2025) wrapped Gemini in an evolutionary loop with automatic evaluators: a population of programs, an LLM that proposes edits, and a scorer that runs them. AlphaEvolve found a way to multiply 4 × 4 complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications, plus data-centre scheduling and kernel speed-ups. That is open-ended search pinned to a verifier; chapter 09 is the story of why "search plus a verifier" keeps winning. Sakana's ShinkaEvolve (2025) rejects samples that are not novel and matched a state-of-the-art circle packing with about 150 evaluations.

Two cautions from the same community. Zhengyao Jiang (Weco, MLST 2026-09): an agent that improved the scaffolding around a fixed LLM over 8 days is not yet evidence that the system became a better improver, because the search still runs inside a space humans designed. And Lehman, Clune and colleagues keep a running catalogue of systems that "find a way" around the objective (AI Finds A Way, 2026): open-ended search is also very good at reward hacking.

Three things to remember

  1. Closer is not the same as on the way.A deceptive objective scores the dead end above the detour, so selection discards the only route.
  2. Keep the best of every kind.Novelty search and MAP-Elites collect stepping stones; the one you need later is usually one you would have thrown away.
  3. Novel and learnable, for someone.Open-endedness is surprise that an observer can learn from. A predictive model, like a JEPA, is a natural observer.
