Part 1 · Predict · Chapter 01

Why does guessing teach anything?

to guess what is hidden, a model has to capture whatever produced it. Hide part of a sentence, an image or a video and the data becomes its own answer key, so prediction is a teacher that never runs out of labels. What the model learns depends on what you ask it to guess, and that choice is the subject of this whole site.

Beat 1

Why is prediction a free teacher?

Because the label is already inside the data. Cover a piece, ask the model to guess it, then uncover it and grade the guess. No human writes the answer, so every sentence and every frame yields many training examples at no cost.

Supervised learning needs someone to say "this photo is a dog". ImageNet needed about 1.3 million such judgments for its training set, and every new task needs new ones. Self-supervised learning removes the human: it splits each example into a visible part and a hidden part and trains the model to get from one to the other. The hidden part is the label.

This is older than deep learning. In 1951 Claude Shannon played a guessing game with people: reveal a text one letter at a time, ask for the next letter, count the guesses. From that he estimated that English carries roughly one bit per letter, far below the 4.7 bits of 27 equally likely symbols. The gap between the two numbers is exactly the structure a good guesser has learned. A model's prediction loss, measured in bits, is the same quantity: every bit it stops losing is a regularity it now knows.

The lab shows how one sentence turns into several exercises. Three recipes, three famous models: hide the next token (GPT), hide random tokens (BERT, 2018), hide a block and predict a summary of it (the JEPA idea from chapter 02).

Beat 2

Why did next-token prediction make LLMs?

Because driving the loss down on the next token of the whole internet requires spelling, grammar, facts, arithmetic and a model of who is writing. Each of those removes some bits of surprise, so the objective keeps rewarding more knowledge as the model and the data grow.

The simplest next-character predictor is a counting table: for each context of the last n−1 characters, count which character came next. That is an n-gram model, and the lab below trains one in your browser. It exposes the central trade-off. Longer contexts predict better, but each long context is seen only once or twice, so the table memorizes instead of generalizing. Counting cannot share what it learned about "the cat sat" with "the dog sat".

A neural language model replaces the table with a function. Tokens become vectors (see attention in the course), similar contexts land near each other, and statistics transfer between them. That is why a transformer can use thousands of tokens of context without needing to have seen any of them before. The objective did not change from Shannon's game. The guesser did.

What a next-token model learns inside can be surprisingly tidy. Shai et al. (2026) found that transformers trained on next-token prediction place conditionally independent factors of their synthetic worlds in orthogonal subspaces. Prediction alone was enough to factor the world, at least there.

Beat 3

Why are there three ways to learn by guessing?

Because there are three things you can ask the model to produce: the hidden raw data (reconstruction), an agreement between two views (alignment), or the hidden part's embedding (latent prediction). Each choice buys one strength and one failure mode.

Dutta, Vyas and Roy (2026) sort self-supervised learning into exactly these three families and compare BYOL, MAE and I-JEPA as one representative each. The table of risks is the useful part: reconstruction never collapses but pays for every detail; alignment and latent prediction choose what to keep but can cheat by mapping everything to one point.

Family 1

Reconstruction

Hide part of the input, regenerate it exactly. The target is raw data, fixed by the world.

GPT (next token) · BERT 2018 · MAE 2021 · diffusion

Risk: spends capacity on whatever varies most, meaningful or not (beat 4).

Family 2

Alignment / contrastive

Two augmented views of one image should embed close together; other images should embed far away (or the embedding must keep its variance).

SimCLR 2020 · BYOL 2020 · Barlow Twins · VICReg · DINO

Risk: collapse, and the invariances are only as good as the hand-made augmentations.

Family 3

Latent prediction

Hide part of the input and predict the hidden part's representation, computed by a learned encoder.

data2vec 2022 · I-JEPA 2023 · V-JEPA 2024 · LeJEPA 2025

Risk: collapse too, which is why chapter 03 exists.

Why does text get away with reconstruction? A token is already an abstraction: a person chose the word, and BPE (see the course's BPE explorable) chose the pieces. Pixels have no such filter. A camera records every speck of sensor noise and every leaf with the same weight as the dog. That asymmetry is the root of the JEPA argument, and the next lab and beat 4 make it concrete.

First, the alignment family in motion. Wang and Isola (2020) showed that the contrastive loss splits into two forces: alignment (pull the two views of each image together) and uniformity (spread all embeddings evenly over the sphere). The lab trains a small encoder with both forces on a circle.

Beat 4

Why does reconstruction learn variance instead of meaning?

Because a reconstruction loss counts squared error, and the cheapest way to cut squared error is to capture the directions where the data varies most. When the loudest direction is noise, a reconstructing model learns noise first. A predictor across views only keeps what the other view can confirm.

Balestriero and LeCun (2024) made this precise for linear auto-encoders and argued it carries over to deep ones: reconstruction ranks features by variance, and in natural images the top-variance subspaces hold texture, lighting and fine detail, which are poorly aligned with perception tasks. To get good features out of an MAE you need a long training run and a fine-tuning step that re-ranks them. Littwin et al. (2024) showed the JEPA side of the story in deep linear networks: a JEPA ranks features by how predictable they are (chapter 04).

The lab is the smallest version of that argument. Each data point has a quiet signal direction that tells two classes apart, and a loud noise direction. Two views of each point share the signal and, by default, have independent noise, the way two crops of one photo share the object but not the speckle. PCA is the optimal linear reconstruction. The cross-view predictor keeps the direction where one view predicts the other (the top eigenvector of the cross-covariance).

Picture it

Reconstruction is a stenographer who writes down everything said in a noisy café, including the espresso machine. Prediction across views is two witnesses comparing notes: the espresso machine was different in each recording, so neither can predict the other's, and it drops out. What they agree on is the conversation.

Beat 5

Why might prediction be intelligence, and why might it not be enough?

Because any agent that reaches varied goals has to contain a model that predicts its world, so prediction looks necessary. Nobody has shown it is sufficient: control, goals, judgement about what is worth predicting, and structure may all have to be added.

"It is prediction"

The case for

  • Free energy principle (Friston): a persisting system minimizes surprise under a generative model; perception, action and learning are all inference.
  • Agüera y Arcas: life and intelligence are predictive computation; LLMs are a real if partial form of it.
  • Brains align with models: Raugel, d'Ascoli, Wyart and King (2025) found LLM layer depth tracks the timing of brain responses, for transformers and state-space models alike.
  • Richens et al. (ICML 2025): an agent that generalizes to multi-step goals with bounded regret must contain a predictive world model, which can be extracted from its policy.
  • LeCun's JEPA: predict, but in an abstract space.
"It is not enough"

The case against

  • Mattick (MLST 2026): prediction is not control; scaled prediction alone does not make intelligence.
  • Hughes (MLST 2026): creativity is not optimization; judgement about what is worth asking is missing.
  • Sutton: imitating human text has no goals and no ground truth.
  • Bengio: build the predictor without agency on purpose (Scientist AI), since agency is where the risk is.
  • Bach, Solms: consciousness needs a self-model or felt needs, not prediction alone.

The disagreement is about what to add. Sutton and Friston add action and control. Hughes adds goals and taste. Chollet and Marcus add structure. Bengio adds nothing, deliberately. The rest of this site follows that fork: chapters 02 to 05 sharpen the predictor, part 3 adds search, verifiers and planning on top of it, and part 4 asks what open-ended search finds that a fixed objective misses.

This projectRunning active inference and a JEPA side by side on one toy environment is on the project's idea list: the same predictive core, two different answers to what goes on top. See chapter 17 and the Why Atlas, question Q13.

Three things to remember

  1. Hide it, and the data grades itself.Self-supervision turns every sentence and frame into labelled exercises, so the teacher scales with the data.
  2. The loudest direction wins.Reconstruction ranks features by variance. When noise is loud, a reconstructing model learns the noise first.
  3. Choose what to guess.Raw data, view agreement or embeddings: the target decides what the model keeps, and the last two can collapse.
