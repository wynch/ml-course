Part 2 · Theory · Chapter 06

Why do networks generalize at all?

training does not pick a random solution among the millions that fit the data. Gradient descent from small weights, weight decay and the architecture all lean toward small, smooth, compressible functions, and for real data the simple fit is usually the right one. Past the point where a model can fit everything, extra parameters give the optimizer more room to find that simple fit, which is why test error can fall a second time.

Beat 1

Why is generalization a puzzle in the first place?

Because the same network that generalizes on real labels can also memorize pure noise. Whatever makes it generalize cannot be a lack of capacity. It has to be a preference for which of the many fitting solutions it ends up in.

The textbook story is a U-curve. Too few parameters and the model underfits (bias). Too many and it fits the noise in the training set (variance). Choose the size at the bottom of the U. Counting arguments back this up: if a model class can label n points in every possible way, then fitting n training points tells you nothing about point n+1.

In 2017 Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals ran the experiment that broke this story for deep learning. They took standard CIFAR-10 and ImageNet architectures and replaced the labels with random ones. The networks still reached 100 percent training accuracy. Train the same network on the true labels and it generalizes well. Same architecture, same capacity, opposite outcomes. Explicit regularization (weight decay, dropout, augmentation) helped a little but was not what made the difference.

So the question shifts from "how big is the model class" to "among all the weight settings that fit the data, which one does training land in, and why is that one good?" Every answer in this chapter is an answer to that second question.

Classical picture

Capacity decides

Fewer parameters than data points. Test error is a U in model size. Interpolating the training set (zero training error) is a sign of overfitting.

What deep nets do

The solution decides

Many more parameters than data points. Training error goes to zero and test error often keeps improving. The model could memorize noise, and does when asked to, but on real data it finds something else.

Beat 2

Why does test error go up, then down again, as the model grows?

Because at exactly as many parameters as data points there is only one way to fit the data, and it has to thread through every noisy point, so its weights explode. Add more parameters and many fits appear. The smallest of them is smooth, and that is the one ridgeless regression and gradient descent return.

Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal (2019) drew the full curve and called it double descent: the classical U up to the interpolation threshold, a peak there, then a second descent. Nakkiran et al. (2019) found the same peak in ResNets and transformers when sweeping width, training epochs, or even the number of samples.

The cleanest place to see it is a random-features model: a two-layer network whose first layer is frozen at random and whose last layer is fitted by least squares. With p features and n data points, the lab fits

f(x) = Σj≤p wj · √(2/p) cos(ωj x + bj), ωj ~ N(0, σ²)
p ≤ n: w = (ΦᵀΦ + λI)⁻¹ Φᵀy p > n: w = Φᵀ(ΦΦᵀ + λI)⁻¹ yWith λ → 0, the right-hand formula is the minimum-norm interpolant: of all weight vectors that fit the data exactly, the one with the smallest ‖w‖. Everything is computed live in your browser, averaged (geometric mean) over 15 independent draws of the random first layer.
Wilson's readingDouble descent and benign overfitting are not special to deep learning: a linear model on random features does it, as above. Wilson (2025) argues the classical tools already cover it once you measure complexity by how much the model prefers simple solutions, not by how many parameters it has. His group also shows generalization bounds that get tighter along compute-optimal LLM scaling (Finzi et al. 2025).
Beat 3

Why does training pick the simple interpolant out of infinitely many?

Because gradient descent started from zero can only move inside the span of the training inputs. It never adds a component the data did not ask for, so it stops at the minimum-norm fit. "Simple" then means whatever the parameterization makes cheap.

For least squares with more features than points, the gradient is Φᵀ(Φw − y), a combination of the rows of Φ. Start at w = 0 and every step stays in the row space. The component of w in the null space (directions that do not change any training prediction) is zero at the start and stays zero forever. The only interpolant with no null-space part is the minimum-norm one. That is a theorem, and it is the simplest case of implicit bias.

Richer models have richer versions. On separable classification data, gradient descent on logistic loss converges in direction to the max-margin solution (Soudry et al. 2018). In matrix factorization it drifts toward low rank (Gunasekar et al. 2017). Neural networks fit low frequencies first (spectral bias, Rahaman et al. 2019), and random networks are already biased toward simple functions before any training (Valle-Pérez, Camargo and Louis 2019). Chapter 04 shows the JEPA version: deep encoders learn the most predictable features first.

Flat minima, and why the idea needs care

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber (1997) argued that minima in wide, flat basins generalize, because you can describe the weights with fewer bits (a minimum-description-length argument). Keskar et al. (2017) found that large-batch SGD lands in sharper minima and generalizes worse, which made "SGD noise finds flat minima" a popular explanation. Dinh et al. (2017) then showed that for ReLU networks you can rescale layers to make any minimum as sharp as you like without changing the function, so raw sharpness cannot be the whole story. The useful idea survives in reparameterization-aware forms, such as PAC-Bayes bounds that ask how much you can perturb the weights before the loss rises.

Beat 4

Why can a network memorize first and generalize thousands of steps later?

Because memorizing and computing the rule both reach zero training loss, but the rule needs much smaller weights. Weight decay keeps shrinking whatever the loss does not need, so it slowly trades the large memorizing solution for the compact algorithm. Without decay there is no pressure to switch.

Power et al. (2022) trained small transformers on tables like a + b mod p with part of the table held out. Training accuracy hit 100 percent early; test accuracy sat at chance for a long time, then jumped. They called it grokking. Nanda et al. (2023) reverse-engineered the trained network: it embeds each number as rotations at a handful of frequencies and computes cos(ω(a + b − c)), which peaks at the right answer c. The memorizing circuit forms first; the Fourier circuit grows underneath it; weight decay then removes the memorization. Liu, Michaud and Tegmark (Omnigrok, 2022) tied the timing to the weight norm: generalizing solutions live at smaller norm, and the delay is how long it takes decay to get there.

The lab trains a real network on a + b mod 31 in your browser: each number gets a learned 64-dimensional embedding, the two are summed, squared element-wise, and read out to 31 logits (the two-layer MLP with quadratic activation that Gromov (2023) used to study grokking analytically). Full-batch AdamW, cross-entropy.

Not only toysHumayun, Balestriero and Baraniuk (2024) report that adversarial robustness in ordinary image classifiers also "groks" long after training accuracy saturates, and track it with the local complexity of the network's spline partition. Kumar et al. (2025) list grokking-style cleanup as one possible route from fractured to unified internals; see chapter 07.
Beat 5

Why isn't a very wide network just a kernel machine?

Because the kernel limit only holds when the weights barely move. Then the features are frozen at their random values, exactly like the random-features lab. Real networks are trained in a regime where the hidden features change, and learning those features is where most of their advantage comes from.

Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler (2018) showed that as width goes to infinity under the standard scaling, gradient descent on a network behaves like kernel regression with a fixed kernel, the neural tangent kernel. That gave the first clean convergence and generalization theory for wide nets, and it explained why overparameterized nets reach zero training loss. Chizat, Oyallon and Bach (2019) named this the lazy regime and showed it is a consequence of scaling, not of width itself: rescale the output and the same network becomes rich.

Lazy networks are kernel machines, so they inherit kernel limits: no new features, no hierarchy, no reuse. Yang and Hu (2021) worked out a parameterization (μP) where infinite-width nets still learn features. Grokking fits this picture: Kumar, Bordelon, Gershman and Pehlevan (2024) describe it as a transition from lazy to rich dynamics, where the early fit uses the initial features and the late generalizing solution builds new ones.

Lazy (NTK) regime

Features frozen, last step linear

Weights move by a vanishing amount. Function = kernel regression with the initial tangent kernel. Analysable, predicts the minimum-norm interpolant in the kernel's norm. Lab 1 is this case with an explicit random layer.

Rich (feature-learning) regime

Features adapt to the task

Hidden units change, become sparse and specialised (the Fourier frequencies in lab 3). Better sample efficiency and transfer. Theory is harder: dynamics are nonlinear and depend on initialization scale, learning rate and depth.

Beat 6

Why are there so many theories, and do they agree?

Because each one explains a different slice: why the chosen solution is simple, how to measure that simplicity, and what the network is doing when it gets there. They mostly agree in-distribution. None of them yet predicts practice well enough to steer it, and none says whether "simple" also means well organized inside.

Occam, made precise

PAC-Bayes and marginal likelihood

Wilson and Izmailov (2020): averaging over posterior modes acts as an automatic Occam's razor. Bounds depend on how compressible the trained weights are, not on parameter count.

Predicts: bigger models can generalize better if their prior favours simple functions more strongly.

Geometry

Spline partitions

Balestriero and Baraniuk (2018): a ReLU net is a continuous piecewise-affine map. Balestriero, Pesenti and LeCun (2021): in high dimension test points almost never lie inside the training set's convex hull, so all learning is extrapolation.

Predicts: local complexity (density of linear regions) tracks memorization and grokking.

Compression

Rate reduction

Yi Ma: intelligence is parsimony (compress to low-dimensional structure) plus self-consistency (a closed loop that regenerates and corrects). ReduNet and CRATE derive layers as unrolled steps of the MCR² objective, landing close to ViT on ImageNet.

Predicts: architectures can be derived instead of searched, and are interpretable by construction.

Singular learning theory

Degenerate minima count less

Watanabe: neural nets are singular models, so the usual "k parameters cost (k/2) log n" is wrong. The Bayesian free energy is about nLₙ + λ log n, where the learning coefficient λ measures the effective dimension of the solution set. Lau et al. (2023) estimate a local λ for real networks.

Predicts: training phases (like grokking) show up as drops in the local learning coefficient.

Alexander Mattick's complaint on MLST (2026-09) is the honest summary: no single theory predicts practice well enough to guide it. Wilson's view is the most conservative and best supported for in-distribution generalization. Out of distribution, the question turns into whether different models learn the same thing (chapter 07) and whether skill is intelligence (chapter 09).

One open question links this chapter to the next. Does a simplicity bias favour unified solutions or just small ones? The FER paper's SGD-trained skull is not bigger than the evolved one. It has the same architecture and the same output; it is organized worse. Norm and compressibility do not see that difference.

This projectNotebook 01 (drive versus decay) gives a closed-form threshold for which directions a linear JEPA learns: weight decay removes weak directions. Grokking says weight decay can also reorganize a solution. The landscape notes propose the bridge: retrain the FER skull with SGD for much longer under weight decay and check whether symmetry moves into early layers. Kumar et al. name grokking as a possible cleanup route; no one has tested it.

Three things to remember

  1. The fit is not the question; the choice is.A network that can memorize noise still generalizes on real data because training prefers some interpolants over others.
  2. Barely enough is the worst.At p = n there is one forced fit through every noisy point. More parameters make room for a smaller, smoother fit, so error falls again.
  3. Decay chooses, it does not fit.In grokking, training loss is already zero. Weight decay slowly swaps the large memorizing solution for the compact algorithm.
