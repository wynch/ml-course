Part 3 · Machines · Chapter 10

Why do transformers fail at counting?

a decoder-only transformer has to squeeze everything it read into the last token's vector through softmax averages. Two long inputs that differ by one repeated token average to almost the same vector, and in 16-bit arithmetic they become exactly the same. The alternatives fail differently: a recurrent model keeps a fixed-size state that never grows and therefore forgets. Every architecture of 2025-26 is a trade between those two failures, and the same tension decides why models stop learning after training and why tiny models that loop can beat big ones that don't.

Beat 1

Why can't a transformer tell 300 ones from 301?

Because the last token sees the sequence through a weighted average, and adding one more identical token changes that average by about 1/n². Within a few dozen tokens, the change is smaller than the gap between neighbouring 16-bit numbers, and the two inputs get the same representation.

Federico Barbero, Petar Veličković and colleagues called this over-squashing in Transformers need glasses! (NeurIPS 2024). In a decoder-only model, all information about the prefix that reaches the output has to flow into the final token. Sequences like "1 1 1 … 1" of slightly different lengths converge to near-identical final-token vectors, and low-precision floats round them together. That explains failures at counting and at copying long repetitive strings. The lineage is graph learning: Topping, Bronstein and colleagues (2021) linked over-squashing in GNNs to bottlenecks in the graph, and a causal transformer is a fully connected graph network that inherits the problem.

A second result tightens the screw. Softmax is not enough (Veličković, Perivolaropoulos, Barbero, Pascanu, ICML 2025) proves that with bounded logits, softmax attention must disperse as the input grows:

weight on the one right token = eΔ / (eΔ + n − 1) → 0 as n → ∞Δ: how much higher the right token's logit is than the others. Logits are bounded by the weights and the layer norms, so Δ cannot grow with n, and sharp retrieval fades with length. The paper proposes an adaptive temperature at inference.
Beat 2

Why does attention's memory grow while a recurrent state stays fixed?

Because attention keeps every past token's key and value so any of them can be looked up exactly, and that cache grows with every token. A recurrent model folds each token into a state of fixed size, so its memory and its cost per token never grow, at the price of having to decide what to forget.

Generating one token with attention means reading the whole KV cache: two vectors per past token, per layer, per key-value head. Prefill (processing the prompt) costs time quadratic in its length. A state-space model or linear-attention layer replaces the cache with a matrix it updates in place: S4 (Gu, Goel, Ré, 2021), Mamba (Gu and Dao, 2023) with input-dependent "selective" updates, Mamba-2 (2024), Griffin and RecurrentGemma at DeepMind (2024), xLSTM's matrix memory (Hochreiter's group, 2024). Generation becomes constant memory and constant time per token.

KV cache = 2 · L · hkv · dhead · T · bytes state = L · dinner · N · bytesL layers, hkv key-value heads, dhead head size, T tokens. For an SSM, dinner channels each keep an N-dimensional state. The lab uses a Llama-3-8B-shaped model (32 layers, 8 KV heads of 128) and a Mamba-style state with dinner = 8192, N = 16.
Beat 3

Why does a fixed-size state forget?

Because a d × d matrix can store at most about d independent key-value pairs cleanly. Write more, and new memories overwrite old ones. Attention never overwrites: it just keeps a bigger cache.

Linear attention stores pairs by adding outer products, S ← S + v kᵀ, and recalls with S q. If the keys are orthogonal, recall is exact; in d dimensions there are only d orthogonal directions, so beyond about d pairs the keys overlap and recalls get mixed. The delta rule used by DeltaNet and Gated DeltaNet (the linear layers in Qwen3-Next and Qwen3.5) writes only the error, S ← S + β(v − S k)kᵀ, so writing a key twice does not double it and a new value corrects a stale one. It still cannot beat the capacity of d × d numbers. This is why hybrids keep some full-attention layers, and why MiniMax kept full attention in M2 on purpose: they reported that efficient variants hurt multi-hop and agentic reliability.

Picture it

Attention is a library that keeps every book and grows a shelf per day. A recurrent state is a suitcase: fixed size, light enough to carry anywhere, and every new thing you pack presses on what's already inside.

Beat 4

Is architecture still a lever, or did scale win?

It is a lever again. By 2025-26 flagship open models mix linear and full attention, the residual stream became a design surface, recurrence came back, and diffusion language models shipped. The transformer stayed central for so long partly because it fits GPUs, not only because it is best.

Sara Hooker's Hardware Lottery (2020) argues that ideas win by fitting the hardware and software of their day. Llion Jones, a co-author of the transformer paper, told MLST in 2025 that the field is stuck in a local optimum held in place by herding. The structural camp (Barbero, Veličković, Hochreiter, Jones, Hooker) says scale alone will not fix counting, copying and size generalisation. What changed in 2025-26 is that the alternatives started shipping at scale.

Beat 5

Why can't models keep learning after training?

Because gradient descent on new data moves every weight that helps the new loss, including weights the old skills depend on, and nothing in the new loss says to protect them. Old abilities are overwritten: catastrophic forgetting. So deployed models are frozen.

Catastrophic forgetting has been known since McCloskey and Cohen (1989). Sutton's group added a second problem: networks trained for long on changing data also lose plasticity, the ability to learn at all (Nature, 2024 check). The classic fixes are in the lab below. Replay mixes stored old examples into new training. Elastic weight consolidation (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017) penalises moving weights that the old task's Fisher information marks as important. Sparse updates only touch the units the new data uses most.

The 2025-26 answers move memory into the architecture or into inference:

  • Titans (Google Research, 2025-01): a neural long-term memory, itself a small MLP, updated at inference by "surprise" (the gradient of its own prediction error), with momentum and weight decay as forgetting. Scales past 2M tokens. MIRAS (2025-04) recasts sequence models as associative memories defined by four choices.
  • Nested Learning and HOPE (NeurIPS 2025): models and optimisers as nested memories updated at different speeds, a "continuum memory system", and HOPE, a self-modifying Titans variant. Google's most explicit bet on continual learning.
  • TTT-E2E (2025-12): a sliding-window transformer keeps training on its own context at test time. It scales with context like full attention, which Mamba 2 and Gated DeltaNet do not, at constant latency (2.7× faster prefill at 128K), but is weak on needle-in-a-haystack.
  • Sparse memory finetuning (Meta, 2025-10): update only the memory-layer slots that new data activates most, which forgets far less than full finetuning or LoRA check. RL's Razor (MIT, 2025): on-policy RL forgets less than supervised finetuning because it stays close in KL to what the model already does check.
FER's angleA fractured inside (chapter 07) should make this worse: a fact learned in one fragment does not reach the duplicate fragments elsewhere. Rawlinson and Kowadlo (2026) cite FER for exactly this. Untested at scale. No deployed frontier model yet learns persistently without forgetting or safety regressions.
Beat 6

Why does a tiny network that loops beat a big one that doesn't?

Because a single forward pass can only move information as far as its depth allows, while a loop can run as many steps as the problem needs, reusing the same weights each time. Compute spent on the problem at hand beats parameters spent memorising in advance.

The ARC Prize 2025 named the theme of the year the refinement loop. TRM (Jolicoeur-Martineau, Samsung SAIL Montréal) is one 2-layer network of about 7M parameters that repeatedly refines a latent and an answer; it reported about 45% on ARC-AGI-1 and about 8% on ARC-AGI-2 and took first place in the paper awards. CompressARC (Liao and Gu, CMU) trains a 76K-parameter network from scratch on each puzzle with a compression objective, no pretraining. The ARC team's ablation of HRM (27M) found the brain-inspired hierarchy was not the source of its score: a plain transformer of the same size came within about 5 points, and the outer refinement loop added about 13.

The lab is the cleanest version of why. Finding the shortest route through a maze needs information to travel from the start along the whole path. A stack of D local (3 × 3) layers can carry it at most D cells in one pass. One local layer applied again and again carries it as far as needed, and can stop when nothing changes.

Hooker's line from the governance debate fits: FLOPs, and parameter counts, are poor proxies for capability once compute can be spent at inference. Whether the pattern holds beyond puzzles and toy control is open; ARC-AGI-3's final results are one test. For this project it is the reason an M5 laptop with 16 GB is not a toy: most of the 2025-26 "small beats big" results are laptop-sized (see chapter 12 and chapter 09).

Three things to remember

  1. Averages can't count.Adding one more identical token moves the last token's average by about 1/n², and bf16 rounds that away after about 20 tokens in one head.
  2. Library or suitcase.Attention keeps every token and its memory grows; a recurrent state stays fixed and forgets past about d pairs. Hybrids buy some of each.
  3. Loops beat layers.One pass reaches as far as its depth. A small network that iterates reaches as far as the problem needs, with the parameters of one layer.
