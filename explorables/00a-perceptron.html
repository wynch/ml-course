The perceptron, one mistake at a time

Module 00a · Origins ← All explorables

A perceptron only ever does one thing: when it gets a point wrong, it adds that point into its weights. Watch the line lurch, then settle — and watch the mistake counter stop far, far short of the bound that says it must.

Three things to try. Replay the module's run to see the real 200 points from modules/00a-perceptron reproduced in your browser, mistake for mistake. Drag the margin down and watch the same algorithm work much harder for the same answer. Then switch to XOR, where no line exists and the weights fall into a four-step loop they never escape.

Nine margin settings × five seeds, 200 points each, retrained from scratch every time — the module's actual output, inlined. Fitting both curves' log–log slopes with the module's own normal equations gives γ−2.11 for the bound against γ−1.00 for the mistakes actually made. The theorem has the right shape for the worst case, and the wrong shape for this data.

The headline run — the one Panel 1 replays — made 12 mistakes in 3 epochs against a bound of 139.57: 8.60% of its allowance, finishing at w = [4.611830, 6.361736, −2.0].

What to notice

  • Almost every example is free. In the module's run, 12 of the 600 examples the perceptron looked at produced an update; the other 588 were tested and waved through. Learning happens only where the model is wrong — and the second pass costs just one more mistake before the third pass is clean.
  • The first update is the data point itself. Starting from w = 0 every example is a mistake, so the first update sets w = y·x exactly. Reset and step once: the weight readout is the first point, sign-flipped if its label is −1. Every later w is a whole-number combination of the points it got wrong — nothing else ever enters.
  • Squeezing the margin costs you, but not quadratically. Drag the margin from 1.2 down to 0.05 and watch both the bound and the mistake count climb — the bound exploding roughly as 1/γ², the mistakes crawling up as about 1/γ. A worst-case guarantee is a promise about the worst case, not a forecast for your data.
  • The bound is computed before training, not after. R is the longest point; γ is the distance from the origin to the convex hull of the points y·x, found here by the same Frank–Wolfe loop the module uses. Both are properties of the data alone — you can know your worst case before the first update.
  • XOR does not fail slowly — it loops. Switch to XOR and step through: the weights go [0,0,0] → [0,0,−1] → [0,1,0] → [1,1,1] → [0,0,0] and repeat forever, four mistakes every pass. The sum of y·x over the four corners is exactly zero, so one full epoch is the identity. The bound reads ∞ because γ ≤ 0: there is no separator for the theorem to talk about.
  • The fix is one layer away. The module solves XOR with a 2→2→1 tanh network — nine parameters, 4/4 correct, final loss 2.5×10−32. The hidden layer bends the plane until the two +1 corners stop being diagonal neighbours; the output unit is still just a threshold on a line.
