What to notice

  • The spike is not at a "too complicated" degree — it is at p = n. Twenty training points, so the blow-up lands on degree 19, where the model has exactly 20 coefficients. Step to 18 and 20 and watch the error drop on both sides. Nothing about degree 19 is special as a curve; it is special as a count.
  • Watch ‖β‖₂, not the error. At the threshold it reaches 1.26e+06. By degree 30 it is back to 2.45, and at degree 80 (past this slider) it is 0.70. Past p = n there are infinitely many exact interpolants and pinv takes the shortest one — that freedom is what makes the far side safe, and at p = n exactly there is no freedom at all.
  • Train error tells you nothing after degree 19. It is already float noise — of order 1e−18, and the exact digits differ between numpy and this browser because there are none left to agree on — at the threshold, and machine zero for every higher degree, while test error swings across twelve orders of magnitude. Turn the train curve on and follow the red line off the bottom of the chart.
  • The second descent is real, and it does not win. It bottoms out around 0.48 at degree 47, still worse than degree 3's 0.0183. Double descent that beats the classical minimum needs a richer model class than minimum-norm polynomials — that is the honest version of the story.
  • The lift is not a metaphor. Drag the lift slider with the real quadratic map selected and the rings separate into two clouds that an ordinary flat plane cuts. That plane is Σ αᵢyᵢφ(xᵢ) from the module's own fit — four support vectors out of 120 — and pulling it back to 2D gives the ellipse you see at lift = 0.
  • The two lifts are different kernels. The picture-book map keeps x₁ and x₂ and adds a height; the real quadratic map throws the original coordinates away. Both separate the rings, only the second one is what (aᵀb)² is secretly doing.
