Jev: a model that answers in numbers, not text

source created 2026-09-26 updated 2026-10-03 decision-models classification inference-economics retrieval evaluation interpretability bias

Author: Simon Willison (blog post) Published: 2026-09-21, with an update dated 2026-09-22 Raw copy: raw/web-2026-simonwillison-jev-decision-models.md (captured 2026-09-26, full text)

Every claim below cites the raw file as R, with the post's section. The post has no heading before its first section; that part is cited as "opening".

This is a user's first-week report on a new model, not a paper. It gives no benchmark numbers, no architecture and no training method.

What Jev is

  • Jev is the first of what TypeSafe AI calls "System One models". Willison prefers Maggie Appleton's name, "decision models" (R, opening).
  • It takes text in and gives floating point numbers out, not text: categories, yes/no answers, ratings and confidence scores (R, opening).
  • TypeSafe's own line, as quoted: "unstructured state in, typed probabilistic decisions out" (R, opening).
  • The input is a "state": a string, a list of strings or a set of name-value pairs, such as an article or a customer record. One or more questions go with it; each gets its own reply (R, opening).

Three kinds of question

KindWhat comes backCite
Yes/no ("Noul", short for Bernoulli, per TypeSafe's CEO on Hacker News)one number between 0 and 1: how confident the model is that the statement is trueR, opening
Choice among given optionsa confidence score plus a probability distribution over all optionsR, opening
Score against numbered levels with descriptionsa number somewhere along that rangeR, opening

Speed and price

  • One state can carry as many questions as fit in the context window. The questions run in parallel, so many cost about the same time as one (R, opening).
  • Jev charges for input only; output is free. The first model's input price is $0.042 per million tokens, below GPT-5 Nano's $0.05 per million (R, opening).
  • Hundreds or thousands of trial prompts cost "just a few cents" (R, §"Black boxes are back in fashion").

Strengths and limits, as reported

  • TypeSafe's "Jev 1.13 jaggedness" page says it is weak on numbers, dates and "adversarial content" (R, opening).
  • Willison sees it fit any task that can be put as classification: spam detection, labels, priority, ranking (R, opening).
  • He uses it to rerank search results: BM25 fetches 100 likely matches, then Jev scores each for relevance to the query (R, opening).

Black box and bias

  • An LLM can at least be asked to justify an answer, though the answer may be wrong; Jev returns only a number, so nothing says which signals led to, say, a spam verdict (R, §"Black boxes are back in fashion").
  • He warns against using it to rank job applicants: the number could hide bias that is hard to tease out by experiment (R, §"Black boxes are back in fashion").
  • One test: asked "Good city?" of every Bay Area city, it put Cupertino top and East Palo Alto bottom (R, §"Black boxes are back in fashion").
  • His conclusion: evals and structured experiments matter even more here than with ordinary LLMs (R, §"Black boxes are back in fashion").

What others built in the first week

  • jevchat makes a chat model by asking Jev, symbol by symbol, which symbol comes next; Willison calls the result "terrible" (R, §"Unconventional uses for Jev").
  • jev-leftpad does left-pad with a choice question whose options run from "0 spaces are needed" to "10 spaces are needed"; jev-2048 plays the 2048 puzzle (R, §"Unconventional uses for Jev").
  • Kev tries to rebuild a Jev-like model on Qwen 3.5 at 0.8B, 4B and 9B. A "JevBench" benchmark already compares "Jev-class decision models" (R, §"Open weight recreations").
  • Willison's llm-typesafe plugin adds Jev to his llm tool (R, §"Using Jev from LLM").

What the post does not say

The post says nothing on how Jev is trained, what data it saw, its architecture or its size. It gives no accuracy figures. It never uses the word "calibration": it calls the outputs confidence scores and probabilities but does not test whether a 0.8 is right eight times in ten. Claims about Jev's training found elsewhere are not used here.

Later source (2026-10-03): source-raschka-text-classification-to-jev quotes TypeSafe's CEO, "100% of our data is synthetic", and TypeSafe's blog naming a new architecture and a training method called Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), not public (raw/web-2026-raschka-classifier-history-and-jev.md, §5). It reports 96.47% on IMDb (raw/web-2026-raschka-classifier-history-and-jev.md, §3.3). It too does not test calibration; it reports TypeSafe's documentation calling the probabilities well calibrated (raw/web-2026-raschka-classifier-history-and-jev.md, §3.3).
