Language model harnesses are compositional generalizers

Summary

A harness is the program between the world and the network. It encodes the environment state into one or more LM inputs and turns outputs into the next action (R, §"The capacity for compositional generalization can live in the harness"). The post argues that Transformers are poor at compositional generalization — solving unseen problems by composing familiar ones — and that this capacity can largely live in the harness instead (R, §"Better scaling requires compositional generalization"; §"The Scaling Picture and Moving Forward"). It tests the claim by RL-training a Recursive Language Model (source-recursive-language-models) and a plain Transformer on the same tasks (R, §Intro).

Key claims

  • Locally in-distribution (LID). A good harness shapes each LM call so its prompt is in-distribution with the training data, even when the whole task is out-of-distribution (R, §"The capacity for compositional generalization can live in the harness", Figure 4).
  • Equivalence operator. A harness induces an equivalence relation over task states. Structurally similar tasks fall in one class and give the network similar observations. The learnable space shrinks to the quotient set (R, same section; footnote 1 on why the classes need an epsilon-ball to stay transitive).
  • Claude Code and Codex break LID. They append task data, tool outputs and reasoning to one growing context, which drifts out of distribution: "context rot" (R, §"The capacity for compositional generalization can live in the harness").
  • Two mechanisms keep LID (R, §"Equivalence classes over trajectories induced by the RLM harness"):
    1. Context offloading — the input lives in a REPL variable, not in the root prompt. On its own it is not enough: sub-agent results flowing back still break LID over long runs.
    2. Programmatic sub-agent calls — tools and sub-agents are functions in the REPL, so their outputs sit in variables the root model never reads. The post rates this "equally as important" as offloading.
  • Length generalization. Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507, trained as an RLM on short splits of six tasks (MRCRv2, GraphWalks, LongBenchPro, OOLONG, OOLONG-Pairs, Ada-LEval), improves on splits 8–32× longer. Training: 150 steps, batch 64, 4 rollouts each, prime-rl (decoupled PPO, GRPO-like advantages, KL loss). The same model trained as a plain Transformer (+ YaRN) raises its train reward, often above the RLM's, while its long eval stays flat (R, §"RLMs can train on short tasks and generalize to longer, unseen tasks"; Figures 1, 6).
  • Summary figure: roughly 10× the eval lift for the same train lift, versus training the Transformer directly (R, §Intro).
  • Where length generalization fails. A short task can be solved by handing the whole input to one sub-call, which does not scale. On MRCRv2 the RLM learned that shortcut; a "nudge to decompose" hint fixed it. How much supervision a harness needs to find the general strategy is open (R, §"RLMs can train on short tasks…", paragraphs "Why length generalization occurs" and the nudge paragraph).
  • Cross-domain transfer. Trained on one domain, evaluated on another with the same decomposition: OOLONG trec-coarse → spam; OBLIQ-Bench writing → math; Twitter stance → Wildchat errors. The RLM transfers; the Transformer barely moves, and its early eval gains came from learning the answer format (R, §"RLMs can generalize to unseen tasks that share latent structure observed in training", Figure 7; 500 steps).
  • Cost. RLM training ran 1.5–3× slower than the plain Transformer on same-size tasks, but the gap should favor the RLM as tasks grow (R, same section, "On the cost of training harnesses").
  • Trajectory similarity. On five distance proxies (token edit distance, 3-gram containment, 3-gram Jaccard, weighted Jaccard, length ratio), RLM eval trajectories sit closer to some past training trajectory than the Transformer's do. The authors say the proxies miss semantic similarity (R, §"Appendix", Figure 8). The RLM still sometimes prints task-specific data back into the root context (R, §"How close are unseen RLM task trajectories to train trajectories?", Remark).
  • Bitter lesson. Hand-coding strategies such as MapReduce into harnesses will lose. Data scaling stays the main driver; the harness and its inductive biases set the coefficients. In future the harness may blur into what we call the architecture (R, §"The Scaling Picture and Moving Forward").
