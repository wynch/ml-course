Summary

The successor to source-pi-zero. The question: can a robot policy clean a kitchen or bedroom in a home it has never seen? (R, §I.)

The answer here is the training mix, not the model. Only about 400 hours come from the target robots, mobile two-armed robots in about 100 homes; 97.6% of the first-phase examples come from elsewhere: fixed arms in many homes, many robot types in a lab (with the open OXE set), subtask labels, and web data for captions, question answering and object boxes (R, §I, §IV-C).

One model does two jobs. Given "clean the bedroom", it first writes the next subtask in words ("pick up pillow"), then produces the motor commands for that subtask (R, §IV-A). Training has two stages: 280k steps with actions as discrete FAST tokens, then 80k steps that add a flow-matching action expert for smooth, fast control, with web data kept in the mix (R, §IV-B, §IV-D). At run time it sends 50-step action chunks at 50 Hz to simple joint controllers, with no motion planning or collision checks (R, §IV-E, Appendix A-E). Sizes match π0: a 2B transformer from PaliGemma plus a 300M action expert (R, Appendix A-E).

Key results

Tests run in homes and mock rooms not seen in training, 10 trials per task and policy, about 40 per policy, scored by rubric (share of steps done) with two-sided t-tests (R, §V, Appendix A-B).

  • New homes. In three real homes, three kitchens and three bedrooms, it succeeds on tasks lasting 2–5 minutes from a one-line command; scores in mock rooms match scores in real homes (R, §V-A, Figure 7).
  • More homes, better generalisation. Training on robot data from 3, 12, 22, 53, 82 and 104 locations, scores rise with the count; at 104 locations the model matches one trained on the test homes themselves (R, §V-B, Figure 8). Without the rest of the mix, training on the test homes or on all 104 locations does "significantly worse" (R, §V-B).
  • Other robots matter most. Removing the fixed-arm data or the lab data cuts scores significantly; removing both cuts more (R, §V-C, Figure 10).
  • Web data matters for new objects, not for the tasks. Removing web data makes no significant difference on the four tasks, but hurts picking objects of unseen kinds by name and hurts subtask choice (R, §V-C, §V-E, Figures 10, 11, 13).
  • The subtask step helps, and most of the help comes from training on it. The full model scores best, above even a human choosing subtasks; second best is the same model run without the subtask step but trained on subtask data. GPT-4 as subtask chooser scores worst (R, §V-E, Figure 13). Spoken step-by-step instructions, about 11% of the subtask examples, are needed: without them scores fall significantly (R, §V-E).
  • Against π0. π0.5 beats π0 and an improved π0 trained with the same token-then-flow recipe on robot data only, even when π0 trains to 300k steps (R, §V-D, Figure 12). It follows instructions much better than π0, which the authors credit to training on discrete tokens (R, Appendix A-C, Figure 15).

Caveats

  • Nearly every result is a chart; the text gives directions and significance, not scores.
  • The four scored tasks are in the training data; only the homes, objects and layouts are new (R, Appendix A-B).
  • Failures the authors list: unfamiliar drawer handles, a spill hidden by the arm, the subtask step being "easily distracted" (opening and closing a drawer over and over), simple prompts only, short context and no memory (R, §VI).
  • Physical Intelligence tests its own model on its own robots; no outside group ran the comparisons.

Connections

  • source-openvla — Contradiction (partial): OpenVLA's lesson "no co-fine-tuning needed" against π0.5's finding that other robots' data and web data drive generalisation to new homes and objects (R, §V-C).
