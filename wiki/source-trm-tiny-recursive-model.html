TRM: Recursive Reasoning with Tiny Networks

Summary

The Hierarchical Reasoning Model (HRM, Wang et al. 2025) beat large language models on Sudoku, mazes and ARC-AGI with 27M parameters and about 1,000 training examples. It ran two small networks at two speeds, justified by brain rhythms and a fixed-point theorem, and trained with deep supervision: up to 16 rounds per example, each starting from the last round's state with the gradient cut (R, Abstract, §2). An outside analysis by the ARC Prize Foundation, as the paper reports it, found deep supervision did most of the work: it doubled accuracy (19% to 39%), while HRM's two-speed recursion added little (35.7% to 39.0%) (R, §1).

TRM keeps deep supervision and strips the rest. One network with two layers carries two things from round to round: the current answer y and a latent "scratch" state z. Each round it updates z six times from the question, the answer and z, then updates y once from y and z; it repeats that three times, with gradients only through the last repeat, but through all of its steps (R, §4.1, §4.2, Figure 3). No fixed point is assumed, and a single halting head replaces HRM's Q-learning, which needed a second forward pass (R, §4.1, §4.6). An average of the weights (EMA, 0.999) keeps training on tiny data from collapsing (R, §4.7).

The paper's reading of HRM's two states is plain: z_H is the current answer and z_L is the reasoning, so two is the right number and the biology is not needed (R, §4.2).

Key results

  • Sudoku-Extreme (1,000 training puzzles, 423,000 test): 87.4% with an attention-free 5M model, against HRM's 55.0%. Every piece matters in the ablation: back to HRM's 1-step gradient, 56.5%; self-attention instead of an MLP over positions, 74.7%; no EMA, 79.9%; 4 layers instead of 2, 79.5%; two networks instead of one, 82.4% (R, §4, Table 1).
  • Smaller is better here. Adding layers or mixture-of-experts layers lowered test accuracy, which the author reads as overfitting on scarce data (R, §4.4, "Ideas that failed").
  • Two states beat one or seven: 87.4% against 71.9% (one) and 77.6% (seven) (R, §4.2, Table 2).
  • Maze-Hard (30×30): 85.3% with a 7M attention model, against HRM's 74.5%; the attention-free variant scores 0.0 on 30×30 grids (R, §5, Table 4).
  • ARC-AGI (public evaluation sets, two tries): 44.6% on ARC-AGI-1 and 7.8% on ARC-AGI-2 with 7M parameters, against HRM's 40.3% and 5.0% at 27M. The abstract rounds these to 45% and 8%. In the same table, Gemini 2.5 Pro 32K scores 37.0% and 4.9%, Grok-4-thinking 66.7% and 16.0% (R, §5, Table 5).
  • Cost. Sudoku runs take under 36 hours on one L40S GPU; ARC runs about three days on four H100s (R, "Hyper-parameters and setup").
  • Award. TRM took the first paper award of ARC Prize 2025, $50k (source-arc-prize-2025-report; raw/arxiv-2601-10904-arc-prize-2025-technical-report.md, Table 2).

Caveats

  • The LLM rows of Tables 4 and 5, and HRM's numbers, are copied from the HRM paper, not rerun (R, "Hyper-parameters and setup"). The Grok-4 rows beat TRM, which is why the abstract says "higher than most LLMs".
  • ARC scores are on the public evaluation sets, not ARC Prize's hidden sets. Each ARC puzzle gets its own learned embedding and the answer is a vote over 1,000 augmentations (R, §5, "Hyper-parameters and setup"); a per-puzzle embedding implies the evaluation puzzles' demonstration pairs are trained on, as in HRM, though the paper does not say this in words.
  • Heavy augmentation does much of the lifting: 1,000 shuffles per Sudoku, 1,000 colour, rotation and shift variants per ARC example (R, §5). source-llm-reasoning-skeptics found augmentation alone took HRM from 54.5% to 96.9% on Sudoku-Extreme.
  • The model gives one deterministic answer; it is supervised learning, not a generative model (R, §6).
  • Why recursion beats a bigger network is left open: "we suspect it has to do with overfitting, but we have no theory" (R, §6).
