Synthesis

The series has one thesis in two halves.

  1. The brain runs a variant of actor-critic model-based RL (P2–P7). He argues that about 90% of the brain (cortex, striatum, cerebellum and more) starts with random-like parameters and learns within a lifetime; the hypothalamus and brainstem hold fixed, gene-written circuits that supply rewards and other training signals. Byrnes names these the Learning and Steering Subsystems. Learned predictors in the striatum ("Thought Assessors") translate any thought into a fixed set of scores the fixed circuits can read; one of those scores is the value function.
  2. So a brain-like AGI would have a reward function its programmers write, and nobody knows what to write (P8–P15). Every goal it forms, even a "higher" one, would trace back to that reward function through learning; the concepts we want it to care about live inside a learned, unlabelled world model that the reward code cannot name directly. Byrnes calls this an open problem and offers research directions, not a solution.

Glossary: actor-critic RL, an agent with a part that proposes actions (actor) and a part that estimates how good they will turn out (critic, or value function); model-based, the agent also has a model of the world it can plan with; reward function, the fixed code that scores outcomes; world model, the agent's learned predictions of how things go.

Main kind of evidence: argument, toy models and pointers to neuroscience papers, none of which this wiki has read. No post reports a measurement or a simulation. Byrnes states his own neuroscience claims may be wrong and lists checking them as a research program (P15, §15.2.1.1). He also says post 6 gives "things that I believe right now", as opposed to neuroscience consensus (P6, §6.1). Posts 12–14 are, in his words, "less well thought out" and "most lightly-held" (P12, §12.1; P14, §14.1).

The wiki's reading: for ML, the useful part is a clean decomposition of an RL agent into (a) a generator of candidate plans, (b) many learned predictors of fixed signals, one of them the value, and (c) fixed code that turns those predictions and its own senses into rewards. It names where alignment can fail: in the code (outer) or in what the predictors learned (inner).

Details worth keeping

Learning from scratch (P2). Defined as: starts out useless, outputting helpful signals no more often than chance, until it learns within the lifetime; not the same as "blank slate", since the algorithm, architecture and hyperparameters are innate (§2.2–§2.3). Neonatal evidence: he quotes reviews saying newborn movement is mainly subcortical (Morton & Johnson 1991; Blumberg & Adolph 2023) (§2.5.2). Pattern separation, in ML terms, is "a very wide hidden layer, at the input side, with fixed weights", random at a small scale; a learner can only use its outputs by learning, so it forces learning from scratch; he suggests evolution used it because errors are easy to learn through one layer and hard through many (§2.5.4). Who agrees: he places connectionists such as O'Reilly, and Hawkins, on his side and evolutionary psychology and "evolutionary pretraining" views against (§2.6). Why it matters for safety: what we want the AGI to want lives "buried inside a big hard-to-interpret learned-from-scratch data structure", so reward code cannot point at it directly (§2.1, §2.7). He warns against "pinning their hopes on interpretability" for a model that keeps learning (§2.7.1).

The motivation loop (P6). Thought Assessors use everything in the cortex, latents included, as inputs, so a thought alone can raise a "will taste sweet" prediction; this "scorecard" is how fixed circuits judge thoughts they cannot understand (§6.2.2). "Model-based RL" here means Thorndike's law of effect plus search, not the Markov decision process of textbooks; brain rewards depend on what the animal is thinking (§6.4.1). Sequential comparison: thoughts are spread across the whole cortex, so two cannot be held at once, as a Hopfield network recalls one memory at a time (§6.5.2). He cites rats replaying both routes at a maze fork (Johnson & Redish 2007) (§6.5.1).

Reward is not the target (P9). Credit assignment "paints" learned concepts with valence (§9.3). An agent with any non-reward concept marked good avoids a strong wireheading drive; avoiding a weak one "seems quite tricky" (§9.4.2). A wireheading AGI is dangerous, not idle: to keep wireheading it must stop people from shutting it down (§9.4.3). He wants credit assignment to converge partly, not fully: full convergence would mean wireheading, none would mean the reward never shapes goals (§9.5.2). Ersatz interpretability: give each reward term its own value head; a plan's score then shows which drive made it attractive. He notes this applies to any RL agent with a multi-dimensional value function (§9.6, §9.6.1).

Behaviorist rewards (P10, §10.2.3). His term for rewards that depend only on visible behaviour and world state, "almost every reward function in the entire RL literature". His "current belief" is that they "universally lead to egregiously misaligned AGIs"; he does not defend it in the post and points to a 2025 post (not captured).
