Sutton — AI Creativity and Discovery

source created 2026-07-04 updated 2026-09-25 reinforcement-learning self-improvement evolutionary emergence foundational debate

Author: Richard S. Sutton (Turing Award; RL pioneer — Keen, UAlberta, Amii) / Date: 2026-05-31 / Type: recorded talk to the SAIR Foundation meeting (full text posted to X)

Summary

Sutton argues, from an RL vantage, that generative AI trained by supervised learning is incapable of making novel discoveries. He opens with the reviewer's joke — "the parts that are good are not novel, and the parts that are novel are not good" — and claims it applies exactly to generative AI (LLMs, image/video models, and even supervised-learned world models). Because their processing is partly stochastic, they produce a variety of trajectories, but any given trajectory is either novel (from randomness) or good (from training data) — never both at the same time. When the AI's output is novel beyond its source material, that is what we call hallucination. This is fine for mimicry-as-utility (faster/cheaper/smaller/copyable), but "devastating" for science and mathematics, which require true creativity.

The missing ingredient he calls Discovery: the three-step loop of Variation → Evaluation → Selective retention — the mechanism shared by evolution by natural selection, the scientific method, operant conditioning, and reinforcement learning ("generate and test"). What generative AI lacks is the Evaluation step: the generator was pre-trained by supervised learning, leaving no way at runtime to evaluate what it generates, so there is no selective retention and therefore no discovery. Novelty "flickers into existence" but, unevaluated, is lost. Systems that do discover — AlphaGo's move 37, AlphaZero, AlphaFold, AlphaProof, Claude-Code — all have this extra evaluation-against-a-goal machinery; they are more than garden-variety generative AI.

He extends the point to backpropagation: it looks incapable of variation because weight updates are deterministic, but random weight initialization is a necessary (one-time) form of variation — and because it happens only once, networks can lose plasticity over time. His group's continual backpropagation (Nature) reinitializes under-used neurons periodically to keep variation and plasticity alive. His closing "call to arms": to get full AI scientists, share explicit goals with systems so they can create, evaluate, discover, and be fully autonomous — "Let's fully automate Creativity and Discovery."

Key Ideas

  • The novel-XOR-good claim. Generative AI's stochastic trajectories are novel or good, never both; novel-beyond-source = hallucination.
  • Discovery = Variation + Evaluation + Selective retention. The engine of evolution, science, operant conditioning, and RL.
  • The missing step is Evaluation. Supervised pre-training leaves no runtime evaluator → no selective retention → no discovery; this is absent in backprop/gradient descent and in world modeling per se.
  • Blind variation. Discovery needs a partly-uninformed ("blind") variation component; fully-determined proposals can't discover.
  • Backprop's hidden variation is random init (one-shot) — hence plasticity loss; continual backpropagation re-injects variation by reinitializing under-used neurons.
  • Call to arms: give AI scientists explicit goals so the create-evaluate-select loop can run autonomously.
