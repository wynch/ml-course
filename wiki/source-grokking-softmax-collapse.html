Summary

Grokking: a network fits its training set, then much later generalises. Two puzzles: why the delay, and why it often fails to happen at all without weight decay (R, §1). The authors answer both with one mechanism.

Once every training example is right, the cheapest way to lower cross-entropy loss is to make every logit bigger, which changes no prediction. They call this naïve loss minimisation (NLM). For networks where scaling the weights scales the output (ReLU MLPs, transformers without biases, and the last layer of most networks), the gradient then lines up with the weights themselves (R, §4.2, Definitions 5–6). Past overfitting, the cosine between update and weights reaches about 0.9 in output layers (R, §4.2, Figure 5).

The logits grow until float32 can no longer tell them apart. In the softmax's sum, the true class's term swamps the rest, the loss rounds to exactly zero, and so does the gradient from that example. They call this softmax collapse (SC) (R, §3.1, Definitions 1–3). Learning stops, sometimes before test accuracy has moved (R, §3.2, Figure 2).

Superseded, 2026-10-23: the line above said "the loss rounds to exactly zero, and so does the gradient from that example". Only the correct class's gradient does; the wrong classes' gradients remain, as R itself states (§3.1, Appendix B.1), though its Figure 2 caption speaks loosely of "the gradient from a large fraction of the samples" going to zero. Barron et al. (2026) measure the cliffs: a margin of about 5.5 in bf16, 16.6 in fp32, 36.7 in fp64; past it the gradients no longer sum to zero (raw/arxiv-2610-31642-a-fix-for-softmax-and-cross.md, §2, Eq. 4; source-barron-softmax-fix).

Key results

The tasks are addition, subtraction and multiplication mod 113, sparse parity and a 200-image MNIST subset, on a two-layer MLP of width 200 and a one-layer transformer with 4 heads, full batch, weight decay 0 unless stated (R, §2).

  • Precision decides when learning stops. With 60% or 70% of the pairs as training data and no weight decay, test accuracy rises, then halts when collapse sets in; float64 halts later than float32, float16 earlier. At 40%, collapse comes before any progress, the usual "no grokking without weight decay" (R, §3.2–3.3, Figure 2).
  • Stop the collapse and grokking returns, with no weight decay. StableMax swaps the softmax's exponential for a ramp, x + 1 above zero and 1/(1 − x) below; with it, the 40% setting groks (R, §3.3, Definition 4, Figure 4). The weight norm grows all the while, so shrinking weights is not needed for grokking (R, §3.3, Figure 4 middle).
  • Remove the scaling and the delay goes. ⊥Grad keeps only the part of each gradient orthogonal to the current weights. ⊥SGD reaches 100% test accuracy on subtraction mod 113 within 400 iterations, with no overfitting phase first (R, §5.1, Definition 7, Figure 6). It beats the best tuned weight decay, with nothing to tune (R, Appendix E, Figure 15).
  • Why weight decay works, on this reading. It pulls weights back along the same direction NLM pushes them; generalisation comes once that pull outweighs the gain from scaling (R, §5.2, Figure 6c). Mean-squared-error loss cannot be lowered forever by scaling, which explains grokking without regularisation in shallow MSE networks (R, §5.2).
  • Less input dimension, no delay. Coding each number as a random 14-bit vector instead of one-hot makes memorising harder; train and test accuracy then rise together (R, §4.1, Figure 4 right).
  • The Fourier circuit needs no weight decay. MLPs that grok with StableMax and no weight decay show sparse Fourier weights like the ones in Nanda et al. The authors call this "very superficial" (R, Appendix C.2, Figure 12).
  • Temperature does not fix it. Softmax at temperature 10⁵ only delays collapse; label smoothing prevents it but stops full generalisation (R, Appendix F, Figure 16).

Caveats

  • Most results are curves; the raw keeps their captions only.
  • The account covers homogeneous or nearly homogeneous models; models with skip connections and biases are argued from experiments, not proven (R, §4.2, §7).
  • Plain gradient descent run long enough hits a second rounding problem: updates too small to change a weight. Adam's scaling masks it (R, Appendix C).
  • Adam's ε can also halt learning at very low losses (R, Appendix I).
