Temporal Difference Learning

concept created 2026-10-24 updated 2026-10-24 reinforcement-learning temporal-difference value-function actor-critic bootstrapping stub

Stub. Temporal difference (TD) learning trains a prediction of the long-run future by pulling today's prediction toward tomorrow's: the target at each step is the reward that just arrived plus the model's own next estimate. The tabular TD(0) update is V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s′) − V(s)], with α the step size and γ the discount, at most 1. In reinforcement learning the prediction is usually the value function, or critic: the expected discounted sum of future rewards from here on. TD(0) and Q-learning train a value estimate toward reward plus the model's estimate of the next state (raw/arxiv-2609-16222-how-i-learned-to-stop-worrying.md, §3, Table 1).

Key ideas

  • Bootstrapping behind a stop-gradient. The target holds the model's own estimate, but the update does not change the model through it. The update is then a semi-gradient, not the gradient of a loss, and training can only stop where the model equals its own target, a fixed point; the term "semi-gradient" comes from TD learning (raw/arxiv-2609-16222-how-i-learned-to-stop-worrying.md, §1–§3). See concept-stop-gradient, source-stopgrad-regression-flow-maps.
  • Not only for reward. Byrnes calls TD "a general-purpose algorithm that takes a quantity X and spits out a long-term expectation of X" (raw/web-2022-byrnes-brain-like-agi-safety-05-long-term-predictor-td.md, §5.2.1.2).
  • Credit travels back one step per trial in tabular TD(0), fully so only with α = 1 (slower with α < 1), so long delays are slow to learn. TD(λ), eligibility traces and n-step returns are the standard ways to bridge long delays (textbook RL; no source in raw); Dreamer's critic uses a TD(λ)-style return (below). Separately, Byrnes argues that features which stay active from plan to outcome let one update bridge the delay (same file, §5.2.1.2). That is his argument; no simulation is reported.
  • Sum or next reward. Textbook value is the expected discounted sum of future rewards; Byrnes proposes the brain's value is closer to the expected next reward, and notes the two agree when the only reward comes at the end and there is no discount, as in Go; he sets discounting aside (same file, §5.3.2.3).
