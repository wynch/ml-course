There Will Be a Scientific Theory of Deep Learning

source created 2026-09-10 updated 2026-09-10 deep-learning-theory learning-mechanics training-dynamics scaling-laws mechanistic-interpretability position-paper 2026

Authors: Jamie Simon, Daniel Kunin, Alexander Atanasov + 11 others · arXiv: 2604.21691v1 · Date: 2026-04-23 Site: learningmechanics.pub

Summary

A position paper, not an experiment. Fourteen theorists argue that a theory of deep learning is already forming, and give it a name: learning mechanics — a physics-style account of how training moves aggregate quantities, rather than an account of individual weights. They survey five strands they see converging: solvable toy settings, tractable limits (behaviour as width or depth goes to infinity), simple empirical laws, theories that isolate hyperparameters, and universal behaviours shared across architectures. The shared traits they claim across all five: attention to training dynamics, description of coarse statistics, and falsifiable quantitative prediction. They then answer five standard objections and list ten open problems. Nothing new is measured here; the contribution is the framing and the reading list.

Key claims

  • Five load-bearing strands, each with named anchors. Solvable settings: deep linear networks, the NTK (neural tangent kernel — a fixed-kernel training limit), teacher-student models. Limits: "lazy" (kernel-like) versus "rich" (feature-learning) regimes at infinite width. Empirical laws: power-law scaling, edge of stability (sharpness parking near 2/η), neural collapse (last-layer class features falling into one tight pattern). Hyperparameter theory: μP, a parameterization that transfers learning rate across widths. Universality: matched-seed models of different architectures producing near-identical outputs.
  • The right unit is the aggregate, not the weight. They prefer accurate average-case prediction over rigorous worst-case bounds — an explicit break from statistical learning theory, whose generalization guarantees they treat as the wrong resolution rather than as wrong.
  • Theory and interpretability are complements. Their slogan: mechanistic interpretability is the biology of deep learning; learning mechanics should be its physics.
  • A stated failure list. The programme cannot predict scaling-law exponents from properties of the data, has no unified model of genuinely deep nonlinear learning, and cannot explain progressive sharpening (loss curvature rising during training) in realistic networks. μP is asymptotic and does not explain its own success at finite width.
  • The theory will have regimes of applicability. The authors say up front it will not describe everything, and that "the Discretization Hypothesis" — finite networks as infinite-limit behaviour plus correction terms — is not yet validated.
