Summary

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) gives a model 1 for a checked-correct answer and 0 otherwise, and trains it with GRPO. The authors swap the reward for weaker and weaker ones: majority vote of the model's own answers, a format reward (any boxed answer), a reward for one specific wrong answer, and a coin flip (R, §2.2). The question: how much of RLVR's gain needs a true signal at all?

Key results

  • On Qwen2.5-Math, almost none. MATH-500 gain for Qwen2.5-Math-7B after RLVR: +29.1 points with true rewards, +24.1 rewarding wrong answers, +21.4 with random rewards (R, Abstract, §2.2, Figure 1). Random rewards with any non-zero probability work, from 0.001 to 0.7, at different speeds; probability 0 gives nothing, as it must (R, §2.2, Appendix B).
  • On other models, not. The same rewards give little or nothing, or hurt, on Llama 3.1/3.2 and OLMo 2; general Qwen2.5 models gain from weak rewards but less from random ones; smaller models gain less (R, §3, Figure 3). Models already trained with RL gain little from any reward (R, §3, Appendix J).
  • Why random rewards teach anything: GRPO's clipping. With rewards independent of the answer, the expected update is zero, except for the clip term. Clipping caps how far a token's probability may rise relative to its old value, so a rare token hits the cap quickly while a likely one never does; the net effect pushes up what the model already prefers (R, §4, Eq. 2). Remove clipping by any of three routes and random rewards stop helping on average (R, §4, Figure 4).
  • What gets pushed up, in Qwen-Math: code. Before RL, 65.0% of Qwen2.5-Math-7B's MATH-500 answers reason in Python it cannot run, and those answers are right more often (60.9% against 35.0% for plain text). Under spurious rewards, code use climbs to about 90% within 15 steps, tracking accuracy; under true rewards it rises, then falls as text reasoning improves (R, §5.1–5.2, Table 1, Figure 6).
  • Forcing code directly does the same. Starting each answer with "Let's solve this using Python." lifts Qwen2.5-Math-7B from 49.4% to 64.4%, and drops Llama3.1-8B-Instruct from 36.8% to 15.2% (R, §5.3, Table 2).
  • A warning for the field. Two published methods, test-time RL by majority vote and one-shot RL, show the same pattern: strong on Qwen, weak elsewhere (R, §3, Appendix E). The authors ask that RLVR claims be checked on other model families and against random and format rewards as dummy baselines (R, §1, §7).

Caveats

  • On AIME 2025, written after every model's training cut-off, true rewards clearly win; other rewards give −0.4 to +4.5 points (R, §2.2, Appendix D).
  • Qwen2.5-Math-7B's starting score swings with the prompt, even with prompts unrelated to the task (R, §2.1, Appendix I).
  • Code reasoning is one case study, not the whole explanation (R, §5).
  • The text's AMC gains for format, incorrect and random rewards (13.8, 24.1, 21.4) repeat its MATH-500 gains; the accuracy of plain-text answers is 35.0% in Table 1, 28.0% in §1 and 29% in Appendix B (R, §1, §2.2, Table 1, Appendix B). The page uses Table 1.
  • The authors stress spurious rewards are for analysis, not a training method (R, §2.2, §7).
