Diffusion beats autoregressive when data is scarce

Authors: Mihir Prabhudesai, Mengning Wu (co-leads, equal contribution), Amir Zadeh (Lambda), Katerina Fragkiadaki, Deepak Pathak; Carnegie Mellon University Published: arXiv 2507.15857, v1 2025-07-21, v7 2025-10-26; the HTML carries the NeurIPS paper checklist; project page diffusion-scaling.github.io Raw copy: raw/arxiv-2507-15857-diffusion-beats-autoregressive-in-data-constrained.md (full text of v7 from the arXiv HTML, captured 2026-10-20)

Summary

Earlier work found that masked diffusion language models need up to 16× the compute of left-to-right (autoregressive, AR) models to reach the same loss. Those comparisons trained for one pass over the data. The authors ask whether diffusion needs more compute or only more data (R, §1, §3.1).

They hold the data fixed and repeat it. Same GPT-2-style Transformer with RoPE for both; the AR model uses a causal mask and predicts the next token; the diffusion model hides a random share of tokens and predicts them, as in source-llada (R, §2.1–2.2). English C4, 25M, 50M or 100M unique tokens, up to 800 epochs (80B tokens seen), models from 7M to 2.5B parameters; 200 models in all (R, §2.4, §3). Hyperparameters were tuned for AR models, which, the authors say, may favour AR (R, §2.4).

Key results

  • AR wins early, diffusion wins late. At the one-pass compute-optimal point, AR is far ahead; as epochs grow, AR overfits and diffusion keeps improving. At 100M unique tokens, the best AR model sits at about 50 epochs and the best diffusion model at 500, with lower loss (3.55 against 3.71) (R, §3.1, Figure 2).
  • Repeated data keeps its value. Fitting the data-constrained scaling law of Muennighoff et al., the "half-life" of reused data (R_D*) is about 500 epochs for diffusion against about 31 for AR (R, §3.2, Table 1b). Repeated data is worth almost as much as fresh data for about 4 epochs with AR and about 100 with diffusion (R, §3.2, Figure 5).
  • A crossover rule. The compute at which diffusion starts to win grows as a power law of the unique data, C_crit ∝ U^2.174 (R, §3.3, Figure 6). The authors' summary: short of compute, use AR; short of data, use diffusion (R, §4).
  • Downstream too. At 100M unique tokens, the best diffusion model beats the best AR model on 8 of 9 tasks (PIQA is the exception), e.g. SciQ 68.67 vs 58.05 (R, §3.4, Table 2). At 500M unique tokens, a 2.3B diffusion model trained 130 epochs, stopped while still improving, wins 7 of 9 (R, Appendix 7, Table 3).
  • Why: many orders. Attention dropout and token masking do not stop AR overfitting. Training AR on 16 fixed random orderings of each sequence brings its 100-epoch loss close to diffusion's. The authors read diffusion's random masks as built-in data augmentation over orderings (R, §3.5, Figures 7–8).

Caveats

  • "Wins" means at much more compute: in Table 4 the best AR model used 4.32e18 FLOPs and the best diffusion model 1.24e20, about 29× more (R, Appendix 7, Table 4).
  • Small data (25M to 500M unique tokens), small models, one corpus; the fits cover "a limited range of unique data sizes" (R, §2.4, §11).
  • The two families' loss curves are extrapolated with different entropy terms, so their values are "not directly comparable" in Figure 5 (R, Figure 5 caption).
  • The paper gives R_D* three ways: 512.85 vs 31.93 in §3.2's text, 493.89 vs 31.19 in Table 1b, and "≈500" vs "≈15" in §1, where the AR figure is Muennighoff et al.'s (R, §1, §3.2, Table 1b).
  • Masked diffusion still costs more compute per unit of learning; the authors guess why (every AR token is a target, the ordering never changes) but do not test it (R, §10).
