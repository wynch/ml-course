Quantization from the Ground Up

source created 2026-04-13 updated 2026-10-20 quantization inference llm deployment tutorial

Sam Rose — ngrok blog, 2026-03-25, updated 2026-09-28

A ground-up tutorial on LLM quantization, focused on post-training quantization (PTQ) fundamentals rather than specialized schemes like GPTQ or AWQ. Builds intuition from floating-point representation through symmetric and asymmetric integer quantization, walks through converting bfloat16 weights down to 4-bit and 2-bit representations, and benchmarks the quality cliff.

Two primary approaches

  • Symmetric quantization — scale values around zero into a smaller integer range.
  • Asymmetric quantization — use an offset "zero point" to more efficiently pack the available bits.

So 8-bit is 1.66× faster on M1 Max and 1.33× on H100. GPQA Diamond has 198 questions, so 8-bit beating bfloat16 is noise.

Key insight: degradation is non-linear. 16-bit → 8-bit is almost free; 16-bit → 4-bit adds 4.6–6.4% perplexity; 2-bit falls off a cliff. Measured via perplexity, KL divergence, GPQA Diamond, and direct conversational testing.

Relevance

Practical counterweight to the "scaling-only" story. The wiki's view, not the post's: real deployments already live in 4-bit territory, meaning the effective inference substrate is substantially sparser than the training substrate. Relevant to concept-model-training, concept-developer-tools and concept-inference-economics. Also interesting as a cheap approximation of source-neural-computer-metauto's call for sparser, more addressable substrates — quantization is the dumbest way to reduce representational redundancy, but it works, and it's what everyone actually ships.

Trained in, not added after (2026-10-20)

The post quantises a trained model. source-bitnet-b158 trains with ternary weights (−1, 0, +1; about 1.58 bits) from the start; at 3B it matches the authors' own FP16 LLaMA on perplexity (9.91 against 10.04) and zero-shot average (50.2 against 49.7), with 3.55× less GPU memory (raw/arxiv-2402-17764-the-era-of-1-bit-llms.md, §3, Tables 1–2). So the 2-bit cliff in the table above is a cliff for quantising after training; it says nothing of training at low precision. Not a contradiction: two routes. The BitNet comparison is the authors' own, on one run per size.

A second case, 2026-10-20: Apple ships a 3B model at 2 bits per weight. It trains a full-precision model first, then runs quantisation-aware training through the same stages, and adds LoRA adapters to repair quality. The cost is 3.4 MMLU points (67.8 to 64.4) and 2.8 IFEval points (source-apple-foundation-models-2025; raw/arxiv-2507-13575-apple-intelligence-foundation-language-models-tech.md, §6, Table 3). Again a different route from this post's, so not a contradiction of its 2-bit result.

Why fewer bytes can mean faster (2026-09-26)

Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound: each step reads all weights to make one token (raw/web-2025-aleksagordic-inside-vllm.md, §"Scheduler"). The course's int8 lab gives the same reason for real-engine speedups and shows none at 620K parameters, where its engine is compute-bound and dequantizes at load (course/modules/07-inference-internals/README.md, "The quantization lab"). The speed column above fits that account; this page's source did not state the reason in the summary kept here. See concept-llm-serving.
