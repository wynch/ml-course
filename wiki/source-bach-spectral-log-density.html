Exploding variance of means of exponentials: least-squares to the rescue

Terms, in plain words

TermMeaning here
log-sum-exp$\log \int e^{v(x)}\,dq(x)$: the log of an average of exponentials; the softmax denominator when the set is finite
potential $v$any real-valued function on the data space; the thing being fitted
relative density $dp/dq$how much more likely a point is under $p$ than under $q$
KL divergence$\mathrm{KL}(p|q)=\int \log\frac{dp}{dq}\,dp$: the average log of that ratio under $p$
$f$-divergence$D(p|q)=\int f\big(\frac{dp}{dq}\big)\,dq$ for a convex $f$; KL is $f(t)=t\log t-t+1$
weighted chi-squarethe $f$-divergence with $f(t)=\frac12\frac{(t-1)^2}{\rho t+1-\rho}$, $\rho\in[0,1]$
generalized eigenvalue decompositiona basis $v_i$ with $v_i^\top\Sigma_q v_j=1_{i=j}$ and $\Sigma_p v_i=\lambda_i\Sigma_q v_i$

Summary

Many ML objectives contain a log-sum-exp: normalizing a probabilistic model, a smooth maximum, transformers (through its derivatives), entropy-regularized RL (R, intro). When the integral must be estimated from samples, the estimate is unstable. For $z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $n$ samples give relative squared error $\frac{e^{\sigma^2}-1}{n}$ for $\mathbb E[e^z]$: it shrinks with $n$ but grows exponentially with $\sigma$, and taking the log does not help (R, intro).

The post asks whether one can keep what log-sum-exp is good for while dodging this. Its answer rewrites KL-type problems as a continuum of least-squares problems, solved all at once by one generalized eigenvalue decomposition (R, intro; §"Conclusion").

Key claims

  • Testbed. Estimate $\log\frac{dp}{dq}$ from samples of $p$ and $q$. This is equivalent to estimating KL, since $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\sup_v \int v\,dp+1-\int e^{v}\,dq$ (Eq. 1), or in Donsker-Varadhan form $\sup_v \int v\,dp-\log\int e^{v}\,dq$ (Eq. 2). The usual approach swaps integrals for sample averages, and the average of $e^v$ is the unstable part (R, §"Relative density estimation as a testbed").
  • Weighted chi-square is pure least-squares. $\frac12\frac{(t-1)^2}{\rho t+1-\rho}=\sup_u (t-1)u-\frac12(\rho t+1-\rho)u^2$, so the divergence becomes a quadratic problem in $u$. It is a regression: predict $y=1/\rho$ for points from $p$ (chosen with probability $\rho$) and $y=-1/(1-\rho)$ for points from $q$. The post notes a likeness to noise-contrastive estimation (R, §"Weighted chi-square divergences").
  • KL as a mixture of these. $t\log t-t+1=\int_0^1\frac{(t-1)^2}{\rho t+1-\rho}(1-\rho)\,d\rho$, i.e. $d\nu(\rho)=2(1-\rho)d\rho$. So KL is a weighted sum of chi-square problems over $\rho$, solved "in parallel" (Eqs. 4-6). The same holds for all $\alpha$-divergences, but not total variation. The integral form comes from the theory of operator convex functions (R, §"Extension by integration"). (The wiki checked the identity numerically at $t=0.3, 2, 5$.)
  • Closed form with linear features. With $u(\rho,x)=\theta(\rho)^\top\varphi(x)$, $\theta(\rho)=(\rho\Sigma_p+(1-\rho)\Sigma_q)^{-1}(\mu_p-\mu_q)$, where $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are the first and second moments of the features under $p$ and $q$. Integrating over $\rho$ gives a new divergence $F(p\|q,\varphi)$ (Eq. 7), a lower bound on $D(p\|q)$ (R, §"Linear models with closed-form spectral estimation").
  • One eigen-decomposition. With the generalized eigenpairs of $(\Sigma_p,\Sigma_q)$, $F=\sum_i\big((\mu_p-\mu_q)^\top v_i\big)^2\frac{f(\lambda_i)}{(\lambda_i-1)^2}$. The fitted potentials are quadratic-plus-linear forms in $\varphi(x)$ (same section). The post's definition line writes $\Sigma_p v_i=\lambda_i\Sigma_q v_j$; the index is read here as $i$.
  • Stable where single problems are not. Single problems can blow up near $\rho=0$ or $\rho=1$; after integration the estimate stays stable whenever $f(t)/(t-1)^2$ is bounded, which holds for KL (same section).
  • Cost. $O(m^2n+m^3)$ for $m$ features and $n$ samples; with a learned $r$-dimensional representation, iterations cost $O(r^3+rmn)$ (same section).
  • Bias for variance. Regularizing adds $\lambda I$: ridge regression for every $\rho$. In a high-dimensional Gaussian model that favours the older variational estimator, the variational one wins with many samples, the spectral one with few (R, §"Benefits of spectral estimation", first figure). In 2-D simulations with ReLU random features ($m=512$), spectral KL beats spectral Pearson and the variational estimator, except at large $n$ where spectral KL and variational are the same (same section, second figure).
  • Closed-form softmax regression. Take $p$ as a joint law and $q$ as the product of its marginals: the log-ratio is $\log\frac{p(x_2|x_1)}{p(x_2)}$. For a finite label set this is softmax regression, in closed form. It behaves like softmax regression, not identically, and it is not least-squares on one-hot labels (that is the Pearson case, which leads to canonical correlation analysis and shows "masking", where whole classes vanish) (R, §"Mutual information and conditional estimation").
  • Cheaper than Newton. For $k$ classes and dimension $d$: $O(nd^2k^2+d^3k^3)$ per Newton step for softmax, versus $O(d^2n+kd^3)$ once for the closed form (same section).
  • Feature learning. Maximizing $F$ over the feature map $\varphi$ gives a minorization-maximization algorithm akin to EM (R, §"Feature learning").
  • Outlook. The author sees the framework as a possible replacement for the last layer of neural networks, where cross-entropy and log-sum-exp rule. Rates for KL estimation are in arXiv 2605.10668 (R, §"Conclusion").
  • SDP view. $F$ equals a semidefinite program; its constraint guarantees $w(x)+f^\ast(v(x))\le0$ for all $x$, as in sum-of-squares optimization. His earlier framework needed $\|\varphi(x)\|=1$; this one does not, which eases feature learning (R, §"Playing with SDPs").

Relevance

  • Course. Module 00b fits Gaussian classes with equal covariance and finds a logistic posterior, and uses log-sum-exp in predict_log_proba; module 02 fuses softmax with cross-entropy (course/modules/00b-bayes-knn-pca/README.md, Part 3 and "Where this shows up later"; course/modules/02-neural-networks/README.md). The post targets the same setting (equal-covariance Gaussians, affine log-probabilities) and replaces the iterative fit with linear algebra (R, §"The magic of least-squares").
  • Attention and memory. Log-sum-exp is the energy term of modern Hopfield networks (source-hopfield-is-all-you-need) and the statistic FlashAttention stores (source-flashattention). The post does not address attention beyond naming it.
  • Contrastive learning. The regression view echoes noise-contrastive estimation (R, §"Weighted chi-square divergences"); compare the InfoNCE pages source-ssl-contrastive-foundations and source-contrastive-learning-gaussian-representations.
  • Energy-based models. The unstable normalizer is the partition function problem of concept-energy-based-models.
  • Compass. The spine's softmax entry defines softmax as turning scores into probabilities; this post is about the cost of its denominator when estimated from samples.
