Reconciling Kaplan and Chinchilla

Summary

Two famous studies gave opposite advice on how to spend a training budget. Kaplan (2020): grow parameters as compute^0.73, so mostly make the model bigger. Chinchilla (2022): grow parameters as compute^0.50, so grow model and data equally (R, §1). This paper finds the main cause of the gap: Kaplan counted only the non-embedding parameters, and studied small models, where the embedding table is a large share of the total (R, Abstract, §1).

The argument (R, §2.2, §3):

  1. Total parameters = non-embedding parameters + embedding parameters. Fitted to Chinchilla's own model sizes, the embedding share shrinks as models grow (R, §3, Step 1, Eq. 11; fitted ω = 47,491, exponent 0.34).
  2. Rewrite Chinchilla's loss formula in Kaplan's units and solve for the best model size at each budget. The result is not a power law: its local slope changes with scale, and only at large scale does it return to Chinchilla's (R, §3, Steps 2–3, Eq. 19–20, Figure 3).
  3. Simulate Chinchilla's loss curves over Kaplan's model sizes (790 to 1.58B non-embedding parameters) and fit a power law as Kaplan would. The fitted slope is 0.78 with the Epoch AI constants, 0.74 with Chinchilla's own, against Kaplan's 0.73 (R, §3, Step 4, Figure 5).

So both studies can be right in their own units: Kaplan's law is a local slope at small scale, measured with a parameter count that leaves out the embeddings.

Key results

Their own tiny models (0.8M to 4.6M parameters, BookCorpus, context length 16) (R, §4, Table 1):

FitParameters ∝ compute^a
Chinchilla (reported)0.50
Kaplan (reported)0.73
Same runs, total parameters0.49
Same runs, non-embedding parameters0.74
Total parameters, one learning rate for all, no decay0.58
Total parameters, best learning rate, one cosine decay (Kaplan's scheme)0.46
Total parameters, best learning rate, six decays per size (Chinchilla's scheme)0.49
  • Changing the parameter count moves the exponent by 0.25; changing the training scheme moves it by at most 0.12, and the best-tuned schemes differ by only 0.03 (R, Table 1).
  • Loss against compute: Kaplan reported loss ∝ compute^−0.057; Chinchilla, after subtracting a floor, ∝ compute^−0.155 to −0.178. Redone in Kaplan's units and form, Chinchilla's curves give −0.066 to −0.069 (R, §5, Eq. 40–43). Kaplan's form has no floor term (irreducible loss); that also shrinks the exponent (R, §5, §7).
  • Advice: report total parameters and total compute, and fit loss with a floor term. A single fixed learning rate per model size is enough to measure the allocation exponent (R, §7).

Caveats

  • The main result is an analysis on top of Chinchilla's fitted formula, not a rerun of either study; the training runs are tiny models with 16-token contexts (R, §2.3, §4).
  • Kaplan's model shapes are not published; the paper assumes a fixed width-to-depth ratio of about 40 (R, §3, Eq. 13).
  • The authors call this the "first order" cause. Other differences remain: datasets, position embeddings, tokenizers, warmup, and how FLOPs are counted (R, §7, Limitations).
  • Porian et al. (arXiv 2406.19146), a concurrent large-scale rerun, rank the causes as non-embedding compute first, then a warmup too long for small models, then untuned optimiser settings (as summarised in R, §6; Porian's paper is not in raw).

Contradiction

  • With Chinchilla's own account. Chinchilla suggested that Kaplan's training scheme (one cosine schedule per model) explained the gap, and that its own scheme would lower the exponent; here, moving from Kaplan's scheme to Chinchilla's raises the exponent from 0.46 to 0.49 rather than lowering it, and the parameter count matters far more (R, §1, §4, Table 1). On source-chinchilla-compute-optimal and source-kaplan-scaling-laws.
