Inverse Depth Scaling From Most Layers Being Similar

source created 2026-04-05 updated 2026-10-20 transformers scaling-laws depth architecture

Authors: Yizhou Liu, Sara Kangaslahti, Ziming Liu, Jeff Gore · v1 2026-02-05, v2 2026-05-31 (ICML 2026 camera-ready) arXiv: 2602.05970 (Title corrected 2026-10-19; the page had a made-up one.) Via: @yizhouliu0 on X Full text: raw/arxiv-2602-05970-inverse-depth-scaling-from-most-layers.md (v2, captured 2026-10-20), cited below by section.

Summary

Reports that LLM loss falls in inverse proportion to depth. The authors say the 1/depth law probably comes from ensemble averaging over functionally similar layers, rather than from composing computations or approximating smooth dynamics. They call the ensemble regime "inefficient yet robust" and suggest efficiency may need architectures that "encourage compositional use of depth" (arXiv 2602.05970, abstract). (Corrected 2026-10-19: the page stated the ensemble reading as fact.)

Contradiction (partial): source-neural-depth-scaling finds LLM layers probably act as an averaging ensemble (arXiv 2602.05970); source-neural-low-degree-filtering models depth as composition in a stylised limit (raw/arxiv-2605-13612-neural-low-degree-filtering.md). The settings differ (trained LLMs vs a theory limit), so both can hold.

Key Ideas

  • Loss scales inversely with depth: Loss ~ 1/depth
  • Layers probably act as an ensemble average of updates, not sequential composition (the authors' hedge)
  • Challenges the assumption that deeper = more compositional reasoning
  • Implications for architecture design and depth vs width trade-offs

Added 2026-10-20, from the full text:

  • The fit. A loss law with separate width, depth and data terms, fitted to about 200 points reconstructed from the Chinchilla models, gives a depth exponent of 1.2 ± 0.3 (width 0.98 ± 0.08, data 0.30 ± 0.01) (§2, Eq. 3).
  • The hidden-state evidence. In Pythia models on FineWeb, most tokens are updated by small, even steps through the middle layers; the step shrinks as models get deeper; neighbouring updates are weakly correlated, which argues against smooth dynamics, though the authors call this "not definitive" (§2, Figure 2).
  • Not pure averaging either. First and last layers act differently; causal tracing on Pythia-12b shows two functional groups of layers, with averaging inside each group (§6, Figure 6).
  • Limits, the authors' own: the loss decomposition is proposed, not derived; other mechanisms cannot be ruled out (§6).
