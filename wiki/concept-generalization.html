Generalization

concept created 2026-10-23 updated 2026-10-24 generalization approximation double-descent vc-dimension inductive-bias implicit-bias memorization theory

Generalization is how well a trained model does on data it has not seen, measured as the gap between its loss on new data from the same source (population risk) and its loss on the training set (empirical risk) (L6 = raw/web-2024-mit-6-7960-lec06-generalization.md, slide 3).

The puzzle

Networks fit random labels: on CIFAR-10, Inception reaches 100% training accuracy on random labels (9.78% test, chance), and 100% training with 85.75–89.31% test on real labels (L6, slide 44, Zhang et al. 2017). Classical bounds count the functions a model can represent; a class that fits every labeling makes them vacuous (L6, slides 41–43). Parameter count fails too: past the interpolation threshold, where training error first reaches zero, test error can fall again as models grow, "double descent" (L6, slide 30, Belkin et al. 2019). Isola: how to measure complexity for deep learning is "still an open question" (L6, slide 46).

The classical guarantee: uniform convergence (added 2026-10-24)

Counting bounds rest on uniform convergence: one sample estimates the mean of every function in a class at once, so training error is close to test error for every model in it. For yes/no functions, finite VC dimension d is exactly what is needed, and the sharp sample size is Θ((d + log(1/δ))/ε²) for error ε with probability 1 − δ (raw/arxiv-2610-24336-uniform-convergence-at-optimal-rate-and.md, §1). source-uniform-convergence-optimal-scale (Alon, Hanneke, Moran, Waknine, 2026) proves the same sample size for classes of real values in [−1, 1], with VC dimension replaced by the fat-shattering dimension at scale γ and error γ + ε, the best scale that dimension allows, and shows it cannot be improved in general (Theorem 1.1). It is worst-case theory about function classes; it does not address networks or training. The wiki's reading: it sharpens the counting route, it does not rescue it for nets that fit random labels.

Candidate answers: inductive biases

An inductive bias is any preference, other than fitting the data, that decides which of the many perfect fits training returns (L6, slides 47–49).

BiasClaimSource
Parameter-function mapmost random weight settings give simple functionsL6, slides 50–51 (Valle Pérez et al. 2019)
Depthdeeper nets map inputs to lower-rank embeddings (effective rank: a smooth count of how many directions carry most of the variance), even when linearL6, slides 52–59 (Huh et al. 2023)
Optimizersmall starts give low norm (shown for linear models); finite steps give flat minimaL6, slide 61
Architectureinvariances, equivariances, compositionalityL6, slides 19, 62
Finiteness"Anything finite will look small once you have enough data" (a remembered remark of Sutskever's)L6, slide 65

Other accounts in the wiki: source-litman-generalization-theory claims one mechanism, signal and noise directions of the neural tangent kernel, for double descent, benign overfitting and implicit bias (arXiv 2605.01172, not in raw). concept-grokking collects accounts of generalization that arrives long after memorization.

Approximation is not the bottleneck

On the unit cube, wide shallow ReLU nets approximate any L-Lipschitz function to any accuracy in total (L1) error, at a cost exponential in input dimension (L3, slide 10); deep nets can fit some functions with many fewer neurons, shown by counting kinks, with Telgarsky's papers giving the approximation version (L3, slides 33, 35). The construction behind the first result would memorize if trained: a bump on each training point, "would not generalise" (L3, slide 19). The wiki's reading: what matters is which fit training picks, not whether one exists.
