Distribution Sharpening (Power Sampling)

source created 2026-05-06 updated 2026-10-20 llm-reasoning test-time-compute inference-time sampling rl-alternatives distribution-sharpening 2026

Paper: Xiaotong Ji, Rasul Tutunov, Matthieu Zimmer, Haitham Bou Ammar (Huawei Noah's Ark Lab; Bou Ammar also UCL), Scalable Power Sampling: Unlocking Efficient, Training-Free Reasoning for LLMs via Distribution Sharpening, arXiv 2601.21590, v1 2026-01-29, v2 2026-10-12; accepted at ICML 2026 (abs page). Raw copy: raw/arxiv-2601-21590-scalable-power-sampling-unlocking-efficient-training.md (captured 2026-10-19, full text of v2). Cited below as R. How it reached the wiki: a Medium post by Bou Ammar (its URL reads "we didn't train the model, it started reasoning better anyway") in a private link list. Until 2026-10-19 this page rested on that post alone; it is now rebuilt on the paper. The post is not captured.

Summary

If RL post-training mostly sharpens what the base model already does (source-rl-reasoning-boundary-yue), one should get the same gain by sampling the base model in a sharper way, with no training and no reward (R, §1).

The sharper distribution is the power distribution: weight each whole answer by p(answer)^α, with α = 4 here. This is not the same as lowering the temperature. Low temperature sharpens each next token on its own; the power distribution judges whole answers, so it can prefer a token that is less likely now but leads to better continuations (R, §2). Karan & Du (arXiv 2510.14901) sampled it with MCMC, which is slow (R, §2).

The paper's result is a decomposition: the power distribution at each step equals the low-temperature distribution times a factor that measures how good the futures after each candidate token are (R, §3, Theorem 3.1). That factor can be estimated with short random rollouts.

Method

  1. Keep the top K = 8 candidate next tokens from the base model's logits.
  2. For each, run M = 8 Monte Carlo rollouts, possibly cut to a fixed horizon.
  3. From them, estimate each candidate's future-quality factor.
  4. Correct the estimate's bias with a jackknife estimator.
  5. Sample the next token from the corrected probabilities (R, §3.3, Algorithm 1, §4.2).

Key results

  • A toy case. A model that prefers to GUESS (p = 0.6) over PLAN (0.4), where planning answers correctly far more often: low temperature at α = 4 picks GUESS 83.5% of the time; the power distribution puts 55% on the correct planned answer (R, §4.1).
  • Pass@1 on three 7B models (MATH500, HumanEval, GPQA-diamond): power sampling beats plain decoding, low temperature, best-of-N by the model's own likelihood and MCMC power sampling almost everywhere. Against GRPO trained on MATH: GRPO leads on MATH500 for every model (0.740 against 0.708; 0.785 against 0.758; 0.492 against 0.464), power sampling leads on HumanEval for the two Qwen models (0.756 against 0.561; 0.604 against 0.537) and on GPQA for two of the three (R, Table 1).
  • On a model already trained with GRPO (DeepSeek-Math-7B-RL), power sampling still adds a little: MATH500 0.492 to 0.502, GPQA 0.333 to 0.364; low temperature hurts it (0.412 on MATH500) (R, Table 2).
  • Diversity. GRPO's pass@k flattens as k grows; power sampling stays near the base model at k ≥ 8 and above it at small k. Duplicate answers over 8 samples: 83.3% for GRPO, 62.5% for power sampling (R, §4.3, Figure 3).
  • Cost. Against MCMC, up to about 10× faster (2.5 against 0.22 minutes per prompt on Qwen2.5-Math-7B, MATH500), 2.5× at the closest (R, §4.2). Against plain decoding, 2.5 to 3.5 times slower (R, Appendix C.1).
  • 32B. On Qwen2.5-32B it beats MCMC (HumanEval 0.793 against 0.567) at 0.36 against 4.1 minutes per prompt; no GRPO model at that size (R, Table 3).
  • Sensitive to α. α of 4 or 5 works best; 1 (no sharpening) and 8 do worse. Dropping the jackknife costs 4.29 points on average (R, §4.3).

Caveats

  • "Matches or surpasses one-shot GRPO" (R, Abstract) holds out of domain. In domain, on MATH500, GRPO leads in all three models by 2.7 to 3.2 points, and the conclusion's "matches GRPO on MATH500" (R, §6) overstates Table 1. The GRPO baselines are trained on the MATH split only (R, §4.2).
  • Self-consistency (majority vote over 16 samples) beats power sampling on MATH500 for Qwen2.5-Math-7B (0.789 against 0.758), and ties or comes close elsewhere (R, Table 1).
  • In Table 1, Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-Math-7B have identical base scores on HumanEval (0.329) and GPQA (0.278); the paper does not comment.
  • Maximum length is 3,072 tokens for every method (R, §4.2), short for reasoning models.
  • Not a free lunch: each step costs 64 rollouts before block-level savings, and the method is 2.5 to 3.5 times slower than plain decoding (R, §3.3, Appendix C.1).

Key claims carried from the earlier version of this page

  • Low-temperature sampling fails because it "sharpens local confidence, not global quality" — models commit to early guesses instead of planning. (The Medium post's words; the paper makes the point with the toy case above, R §4.1.)
  • Matches or surpasses one-shot GRPO, on four LLMs (abstract). Superseded, 2026-10-19: the paper's Table 1 has three base models with a GRPO comparison; the fourth model is the GRPO-trained one itself, and in domain GRPO leads (see Caveats).
  • Reduces inference latency by >10× against MCMC power sampling (Karan & Du, arXiv 2510.14901). Superseded, 2026-10-19: "up to" about 10×; 2.5× in the closest case (R, §4.2).
  • Contradiction (partial), 2026-10-19: arXiv 2608.14420 ("More Correct Mass, Worse Answers") reports that global power sampling can lower self-consistency accuracy by up to 18.5 points (abstract; not in raw).
  • Later: Power-SMC, a sequential Monte Carlo version (arXiv 2602.10273; not in raw).
  • Reframes intelligence development: many "training improvements" are inference improvements in disguise. (The Medium post's framing.)
