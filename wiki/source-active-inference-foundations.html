Active Inference Foundations

source created 2026-05-07 updated 2026-09-25 active-inference free-energy-principle expected-free-energy neuroscience message-passing textbook foundational

The two canonical reference works for active inference (AIF): the 2017 process-theory paper that first specified AIF as an algorithm, and the 2022 MIT Press textbook that turned it into a self-contained pedagogical reference. Together they are the citation pair every other AIF source in the wiki rests on.

Active Inference: A Process Theory — Friston, FitzGerald, Rigoli, Schwartenbeck, Pezzulo 2017

Neural Computation 29(1):1–49.

Where earlier Friston work introduced the free energy principle (FEP) as a normative claim about what self-organising systems must do, this paper specifies the process — the algorithm, the message-passing equations, and the neuronal correspondences — by which an agent could actually implement it.

Normative vs process

A normative theory says what a system optimises. The FEP says: living systems minimise (variational) free energy; equivalently, they minimise their long-run surprise relative to a generative model of "the kind of states I should be in." A process theory says how the system does it. This paper provides one: a discrete-time POMDP-style generative model, a variational posterior over states and policies, and explicit update equations. It binds AIF to specific neuroscience predictions about message passing in cortex, dopaminergic reporting of policy precision, and frontal-striatal control loops.

Setup

The agent is given a generative model p(o, s, π) over outcomes o, hidden states s, and policies π (sequences of actions). It maintains an approximate posterior q(s, π) and updates it to minimise variational free energy F = E_q[log q − log p].

Crucially, the prior over policies isn't a free parameter — policies are a priori more probable to the extent that they minimise expected free energy:

p(π) ∝ exp(−G(π))

where G(π) is the expected free energy of pursuing policy π.

The expected free energy decomposition

The paper's most-cited contribution: G(π) decomposes additively into two components.

  • Pragmatic value — expected log probability of preferred outcomes under the policy. The "goal-directed" / utility-like term: pursue policies that lead to states you prefer.
  • Epistemic value — expected information gain about hidden states. The "information-seeking" / curiosity-like term: pursue policies that reduce uncertainty about the world.
G(π) = − pragmatic value − epistemic value

Both fall out of the same variational objective. This is the structural reason AIF agents naturally trade off exploration and exploitation without needing an extrinsic curiosity bonus the way reward-based RL does — exploration is inherent in the objective, not bolted on.

Algorithm and neuronal correspondence

  • State-belief updates — posterior over s_t updated by gradient descent on free energy, formulated as message passing on a factor graph. Maps onto neuronal-population dynamics with biologically plausible time constants.
  • Policy-belief updates — softmax over −G(π) with a precision parameter γ (inverse temperature). Updates to γ are reported by phasic dopamine in the proposed mapping — concrete, testable bridge to neuroscience.
  • Action selection — sample the next action from the marginal q(a_t) = Σ_π q(π) · p(a_t | π). The agent doesn't commit to a policy, it integrates over them.

Simulations in the paper run small POMDPs (T-maze, oddball paradigm, saccadic search) to show the framework reproduces known behavioural and neural phenomena: epistemic foraging, dopamine-like reporting of policy confidence, omission responses, etc.

Tensions

  • Hand-specified generative models vs learned ones. The paper assumes you have a generative model. Most ML doesn't.
  • Tractability. Computing expected free energy over long policies is exponential in horizon; the paper handles this with short horizons, factorised priors, and message-passing approximations.
  • Normative status. Whether minimising expected free energy is the unique correct objective, or just a coherent one, remains debated.

Active Inference: The Free Energy Principle in Mind, Brain, and Behavior — Parr, Pezzulo, Friston 2022

MIT Press, 288 pages. ISBN 9780262045353. Open access via MIT Press Direct.

The textbook reference for active inference: a self-contained walk from "why minimise free energy" through "how to actually simulate it" to "what AIF says about brains and behaviour."

Structure

Part I — Active inference in theory

  • The free energy principle and its motivation; what it means to be a self-organising agent.
  • Generative models: discrete-state POMDPs and continuous-state stochastic differential equation formulations.
  • Variational inference and the variational free energy bound.
  • Expected free energy, decomposed (as in the 2017 process-theory paper) into epistemic and pragmatic value.
  • Policies as probability distributions; the softmax p(π) ∝ exp(−G(π)) parameterisation.
  • Hierarchical / deep generative models: temporal hierarchies, hierarchical state-spaces, factor structure.

Part II — Active inference in practice

  • Concrete algorithms: variational message passing on factor graphs; belief propagation on POMDPs; gradient descent on free energy.
  • Worked simulations with code: T-maze epistemic foraging, oddball paradigms, saccadic visual search, decision making, learning, navigation, hierarchical control.
  • Putative neuronal correspondences: cortical message passing, dopaminergic reporting of policy precision, frontal-striatal action selection, connections to predictive processing in cortex.
  • Applications: psychiatry (computational phenotypes), motor control, social cognition, robotics.

What the textbook adds beyond the 2017 paper

  • Continuous-time formulations. The 2017 paper is discrete-time POMDP. The textbook also covers the continuous case, where AIF makes contact with classical predictive-coding accounts of cortex and with state-space dynamical-systems models.
  • Hierarchical generative models in depth. Treated as a first-class object: how to specify, infer, and act on multi-level temporal/spatial hierarchies. Directly relevant to source-schema-based-active-inference (S-HAI hierarchical AIF) and source-why-learn-if-you-can-infer-active-inference (hierarchical AIF for robot manipulation).
  • Learning vs inference. Clarifies that AIF can learn the generative model (parameters, structure) from data — historically downplayed in early AIF papers, which often hand-specified the model. This is the technical bridge to model-based RL and to the Dreamer line.
  • Pedagogy and code. Worked examples make the framework usable. The MATLAB / Python implementations are the de facto teaching standard.

Why this grouping matters

These two works form the canonical reference pair for AIF. The 2017 paper introduces the algorithm; the 2022 textbook is where the field sends readers to actually learn it. Together they fix the algorithmic vocabulary — expected free energy, epistemic vs pragmatic value, policy precision γ, message passing on a factor graph — used by every downstream AIF source in the wiki. They also make the AIF/model-based-RL bridge explicit: same engine, opposite epistemologies.

Neural↔symbolic bridge

A fully specified generative model — typed states, typed observations, typed policies — runs inside the agent. The "neural" part is the message-passing implementation; the "symbolic" part is the model structure itself. AIF is one of the cleanest examples of a hybrid system in the wiki: explicit structured priors, learned/inferred parameters, principled action selection. The textbook makes this explicit: AIF agents are hybrid neural-symbolic systems, sitting comfortably alongside concept-neural-symbolic-bridge as the Bayesian-modelling neighbour of the field.
