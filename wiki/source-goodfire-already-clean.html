Summary

The essay says the polysemantic neurons that led the field to superposition and sparse autoencoders came from ordinary, fixable problems, not from a new way of learning (R, "The Short Version"). A polysemantic neuron is one that seems to have several unrelated jobs, or none a person can name (R, "The Short Version").

The author separates two meanings of superposition. One, from Toy Models of Superposition, is "more features than dimensions"; he calls that composition and good news. The other, the one he tests, is a learning principle: a network short of room packs unrelated things into one neuron because they seldom collide (R, "Superposition"; App. "A Note on 'Superposition'"; App. "A Note on Features and Definitions"). He finds no sign of the second.

His case has four parts.

  • The old examples were misread. InceptionV1 neuron 4e:55, the "cats and cars" neuron, responds to a V shape; its negative pole is an O, and it feeds a flames neuron (R, "Cars and Animals"). The car-into-dogs circuit that Zoom In gave as its superposition example, which the author found in 2019, shows cars and dogs grouped by shared pose. Feature visualization was "reward hacked" into dogs in late layers (R, "Cars and Animals", footnote 3; App. "Feature Visualization in Later Layers").
  • The rest was tools and architecture. InceptionV1's last layer was hard to read because it had no residual stream or LayerNorm. A ConvNeXt of the same size and data has a readable last layer (R, "A History of Microscope Errors").
  • What remains is "recursive generalization". A neuron grows outward along related branches: an "iy" neuron reaches "diy", then "do it yourself", which has no "iy" (R, "Recursive Generalization"). The author says Multimodal Neurons (2021) saw this first under the name "conjoined features" (R, App. "Relation to Conjoined Features").
  • Short of room, a network makes coarser neurons, not packed ones. Cutting a layer from 480 neurons to 10 gives broad but readable neurons, such as one "curves & loops" neuron (R, "What Neurons Do Under Pressure").

A neuron reads as a gate more than a noun. Its positive and negative poles draw a distinction for later neurons, such as taxis against ambulances (R, "How I Think About Neurons"). The Qwen3-32B counts below are per pole (R, "The Short Version").

On sparse autoencoders (SAEs): on the ConvNeXt layers the author found no building block that the neurons lacked (R, "What Dictionaries Learn"). An SAE splits the V neuron into a car feature and a cat feature, which takes the reader further from what the neuron does (R, "A City vs a Knot"). Dictionaries and probes "belong at the end, not the start" (R, "A City vs a Knot").

In language models, the authors redo Anthropic's medical-diagnosis attribution graph with Qwen3-32B's own neurons. The neurons need no reconstruction loss, while, by the essay's reading, Anthropic's largest transcoder predicts the same next word "only half the time" (R, "Exploring Language Models"). They show a neuron-only attribution graph of Kimi K3, which they call the first for a model of several trillion parameters (R, "Exploring Language Models"). Experts in mixture-of-experts models are "far more specialized than previously reported", and the essay calls experts "an anti-superposition architecture" (R, "Exploring Language Models").

Why would generalization win? The proposed reason is "circuit racing": a broad circuit fires on more inputs, gets more of the learning signal, and starves narrow ones (R, App. "Why Networks Generalize"; App. "More on Circuit Ecology"). It predicts that a rare fact rides on circuits related to its topic, not on unrelated ones (R, App. "Does Scale Help or Hurt Superposition?").

The author ends with a method, not a feature list: "exploratory" study that follows questions through neurons, attention and attribution graphs, done at scale by agents (R, "What I Mean by Clean"; "What a Solution Might Look Like").

Caveats

  • An informal essay and "working hypothesis", not a paper (R, header; "The Short Version"). The authors are "maybe 85% sure", and they say the title "overstates the evidence, because we haven't audited everything" (R, App. "On the Title").
  • The language-model numbers come from "preliminary audits" and move with thresholds: how far into weak activations one looks, and what counts as "one thing" (R, "The Short Version", footnote 1). Details are deferred to a companion paper that was not out on 2026-10-17 (R, header; "Exploring Language Models").
  • The essay links no code and no model weights; a few figures link their own JSON data (R, App. "Looking at the Residual Stream"; App. "Feature Visualization in Later Layers"). The trained ConvNeXts and InceptionV1 retrains can be browsed only through web microscopes and atlases (R, "The Short Version"; "A City vs a Knot"). The interactive figures show stored results; the steering figure, for one, "shows saved text; it does not run the model" (R, "Superposition of the Gaps", capture note). The TensorFlow Playground toy is the one figure that retrains live (R, App. "Toy Models").
  • Agents do much of the labelling, grouping and judging: neuron write-ups scored by a judge (R, "Exploring Language Models", capture note on the gallery), graph groups "grouped by Claude", expert themes judged by agents (R, "Exploring Language Models", figures). The essay offers the near-miss test as one check (R, App. "Explanations That Could Lose").
  • The 100,000 attribution-graph audits are "qualitative evidence"; the author does not trust the tools enough to turn them into a score (R, App. "Audits of Attribution Graphs").
  • The Kimi K3 graph is not audited: "We haven't begun full audits of this scale yet" (R, "Exploring Language Models"). Attention is "mostly outside the scope" (R, "Attention").
  • Some neurons stay unexplained, such as InceptionV1 mixed5a:719 (R, App. "Unresolved Responses"). The authors do not claim every polysemantic neuron is recursive generalization, and have not tested it across a whole model (R, App. "Is Recursive Generalization Hard to Vary?").
  • The author co-wrote several of the works he now revisits: Zoom In, Multimodal Neurons, Curve Detectors and the OpenAI automated interpretability project (R, "References").
