Summary

A history of text classifiers, written to place Jev, then a test of Jev itself. His verdict: Jev is "essentially a text classifier", yet not "just" one, because it works well on very different tasks without fine-tuning (R, opening, §3.1, Conclusion).

Key claims

  • Jev on IMDb: 25,000 test reviews, 96.47% with Choice questions (22 min 24 s, $0.6492), 96.20% with yes/no questions ($0.6345). Runs are not fully repeatable; a second run differed slightly. He warns the IMDb test set may have been in Jev's training data (R, §3.3).
  • What it replaces: the old rule was a cheap LLM for one-off decisions and a fine-tuned classifier for repeated ones; Jev can do both cheaper and faster, though a high-volume, narrow task may still justify fine-tuning (R, §3.3, §6).
  • A Jev-like head is easy to build: score each option by feeding text, instructions and the option's description through a transformer to one output number, then softmax across options; the option count can change without changing the model. Making it work on all kinds of tasks is the hard part, so he did not release his clone (R, §4).
  • Training, as far as is known: TypeSafe's CEO says "100% of our data is synthetic"; TypeSafe's blog names a new architecture and a method called Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD), not public. Raschka guesses a small ModernBERT-like model (R, §5).
  • Calibration explained: among reviews scored about 74% positive, about 74% should be positive. Temperature scaling fits one number T on held-out data and keeps the ranking (R, §5.1). RLCR, a published method he links to RLCD only by guess, rewards c − (q − c)², the Brier penalty: a wrong answer at 0.9 confidence scores −0.81 (R, §5.2). In the RLCR paper, as he reports it, expected calibration error on HotpotQA falls from 0.37 to 0.03 with similar accuracy (R, §5.2).
  • Is it needed? Cross-entropy alone is calibrated at its true optimum, but on finite data a model can keep cutting training loss by growing more confident, so test loss can worsen while accuracy improves (R, §5.3). Adding a Brier loss to his ModernBERT gave only a small gain (R, §5.3).
  • Clones fall short: most open Jev-likes he checked fine-tune ModernBERT or Qwen. Two that readers pointed him to score 82.90% (Contrastive Language Models, which he ran) and 92.33% (Laya) on IMDb, and both fail the Tetris test (R, §6.2, Update 2). OpenAI announced a Jev-like "Decision API" on 2026-09-29 (R, §6.2, Update 1).
  • Uses: spam and priority sorting; inside agent harnesses as a prompt-injection screen, a picker of effort level or skill file, a judge, a file finder (R, §6.1).

Caveats

  • He reports TypeSafe's documentation calling Jev's probabilities well calibrated (R, §3.3) but does not test it. No calibration measure for Jev appears in the post.
  • Many comparisons sit in figures not captured as text (ModernBERT vs Jev timing, GLiNER vs Jev, his Brier results) (raw header).
  • One benchmark, IMDb, with a contamination warning from the author; Tetris is a demo, not a score.
  • The "only" weak task, legal reviews in non-English languages, is hearsay from a reader (R, §6.2).
