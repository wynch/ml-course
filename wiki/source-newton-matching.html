Newton Matching: fine-tuning a diffusion model toward a reward, as Newton's method

source created 2026-10-23 updated 2026-10-23 diffusion flow-matching fine-tuning reinforcement-learning sampling theory optimization 2026

Authors: Zeyang Li, Yunan Wang, Paolo Giaretta, Navid Azizan (MIT) Published: arXiv 2609.05727, v1 2026-09-04 Raw copy: raw/arxiv-2609-05727-newton-matching-for-generative-modeling-a.md (full text of v1 from the arXiv HTML, about 7,700 lines; cited as R, with its section)

This is a long, purely theoretical paper. It has no experiments on trained networks; its only concrete check is an exact calculation for Gaussian distributions (R, §1 item 9, Appendix C). This page keeps to the parts a careful non-specialist can follow: the problem, the main results as stated, and the map of existing methods.

The problem

Diffusion and flow models are trained by regression: noise a real sample, ask the network to predict the clean direction (R, §1, §2.1). Two tasks have no real samples to regress on (R, §1):

  • Fine-tuning toward a reward: shift a pretrained model so that high-reward outputs become likelier, while staying close to the original.
  • Sampling from an energy: produce samples from a distribution known only through a formula for how likely each point is, up to a constant.

Both aim at the same target, π ∝ μ·e^(τr): the reference μ (the pretrained model's output distribution for fine-tuning, the constant 1 for sampling) reweighted by the exponentiated reward r, where τ, the "inverse temperature", sets how hard the reward pulls (R, §1, §3.1).

The claim, plainly

Treat each training stage as one step of Newton's method (an optimisation step that uses curvature, not only slope) toward that target, taken over "canonical" models: for each output distribution, the one network that ordinary diffusion training would produce if real samples existed (R, §1). With the right geometry, the Newton step equals the natural-gradient step (a step sized by how much the output distribution changes, not how much the weights change) and can be learned by the same kind of regression used in ordinary training. It needs no importance sampling (reweighting samples from one distribution to stand for another, which gets noisy when the two differ) and no backpropagation through whole sampling trajectories (R, Abstract, §1, §6).

Main results, as stated

The convergence results are for the "ideal iteration": each stage's update and canonicalisation done exactly, at the level of whole distributions (R, Abstract, §7). The sorting of existing methods below is a separate, algebraic result.

  • Newton step = natural gradient. Measuring distance between distributions with the Fisher–Rao metric (the geometry behind the natural gradient), the curvature (Hessian) of the reverse KL divergence equals the metric itself, so Newton's direction is the negative Fisher–Rao gradient (R, Abstract, §6.2). Reverse KL, KL(model ‖ target), measures how much the model puts probability where the target has little.
  • Each step is driven by a regularised reward. The step at current output distribution ρ is driven by r − (1/τ)·log(ρ/μ): for fine-tuning, the reward minus a penalty for drifting from the pretrained model; for sampling, where μ = 1, the extra term −(1/τ)·log ρ instead rewards spreading probability out (R, Abstract, §3.1, §6.1).
  • Descent and convergence. Reverse KL to the target falls strictly at every step for step sizes 0 < η ≤ τ, unless the target is already reached; under mild conditions the iteration converges to the target; with the full step η = τ, convergence near the target is quadratic, the number of correct digits roughly doubling each step (R, Abstract, §7.1, §7.2).
  • Gaussian check. When current and target distributions are both isotropic Gaussians, each ideal stage gives another Gaussian, and both forward and reverse KL provably go to zero, quadratically near the target (R, §1 item 9, Appendix C).
  • Approximations sorted by whether they keep the target. An approximate update is "critical-point consistent" if it stops moving exactly when the model has reached the target (R, Abstract, §9). The paper sorts existing methods by this test (R, §10, Table 2):

  • A warning on stop-gradient losses, for reward fine-tuning and sampling. Several of these methods are written as stop-gradient losses. Such a loss encodes an update through its fixed point and implicitly treats the current network as canonical, which training does not guarantee; the paper adds that the stop-gradient trick "inevitably introduces approximations, even when applied to the exact tangential-update formula" (R, §9.4, §10).

Caveats

  • No experiments: no image, molecule or language model is fine-tuned, so nothing here says how much the exact or approximate versions help in practice.
  • Every convergence result is for the ideal iteration and rests on smoothness assumptions ("compatible smooth-realization") stated in the paper (R, Abstract, §4, Appendix A).
  • The paper's Table 2 placements are its own derivations (R, Appendix I); the authors of those methods may frame them differently.
