ALOHA / ACT — Action Chunking with Transformers

source created 2026-05-07 updated 2026-05-07 robotics imitation-learning transformers bimanual 2023

Zhao, Kumar, Levine, Finn — arXiv 2304.13705, RSS 2023 (Stanford / UC Berkeley / Google)

Two contributions in one paper:

  1. ALOHA, a $20k bimanual teleoperation setup using two pairs of low-cost arms — a leader-follower rig where a human operator's motions are mirrored to the robot, yielding high-quality demonstration data for delicate manipulation (slotting a battery, opening a ziploc, hanging a cup, threading cable ties).
  2. ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), a transformer-based imitation policy that predicts a chunk of future actions at once, then executes them with temporal ensembling.

Together: a recipe for dexterous bimanual behaviour from ~50 human demonstrations that became the de facto baseline for low-cost imitation learning.

Method (ACT)

  • CVAE-style policy: encoder takes the action chunk + observations at training time, encoder + decoder reconstruct the actions. At test time the encoder is dropped; the decoder generates actions from observations alone, with a sampled latent z.
  • Action chunking: predict T=100 future actions in a single forward pass (~1 second of behaviour at 50 Hz). Much longer horizon than prior BC.
  • Temporal ensembling: at each timestep, multiple overlapping chunks predict the same action; average them. Smooths jitter and absorbs noise from individual predictions.
  • ViT-style image encoder + transformer decoder over action tokens. ~80M parameters total.
  • 4 RGB cameras (front, top, two wrist) + joint positions as input; 14-DoF target joint positions as output (two 7-DoF arms).

Key numbers

  • Hardware cost: ~$20k total (vs $50–100k+ for prior bimanual research platforms). Open-sourced BOM and software.
  • 50 demonstrations per task suffice for tasks like "slotting a battery into a remote" or "opening a ziploc bag" — at 80–90% success rates.
  • ACT outperforms VINN, BC-RNN, IBC on these tasks; comparable to source-diffusion-policy but cheaper per inference (no denoising loop).

Why it matters

ALOHA/ACT is the hardware-and-method package that lowered the cost of doing serious imitation-learning research by an order of magnitude. The platform spawned a research ecosystem: Mobile ALOHA (whole-body bimanual on a wheeled base), ALOHA 2 (improved), and the Open-X / RT-X / source-openvla data collection effort heavily relied on ALOHA-class hardware. Pi-zero (source-pi-zero) and other commercial robotics startups directly use ALOHA-style teleoperation for their demonstration pipelines.

Three durable ideas:

  1. Action chunking solves idle-state compounding error. Predicting one action at a time accumulates error in pause states (the policy sees a near-identical observation, predicts near-zero action, gets stuck). Predicting a committed sequence breaks this loop. source-diffusion-policy reaches the same conclusion through a different route.
  2. Temporal ensembling is a free win. Overlapping chunks averaged at each timestep provide implicit smoothing without explicit filtering — a trick that has propagated through the whole imitation-learning subfield.
  3. Hardware democratisation matters for the science. Before ALOHA, dexterous bimanual research required $100k+ rigs; afterwards, well-funded labs and even individuals could replicate the work. The Diffusion Policy / ACT / OpenVLA / Pi-zero ecosystem moved much faster because of the shared hardware substrate.

In the bigger picture for concept-vla-robot-foundation-models: ACT sits at the small-model, task-specific end of the spectrum opposite source-rt-2 and source-openvla. The current frontier (Pi-zero) explicitly fuses ACT's chunking head with a foundation-model VLM backbone — combining the two camps' strengths.
