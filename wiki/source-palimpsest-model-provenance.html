Palimpsestic memorization and blackbox model provenance

Blog post: Christopher Potts (Stanford), "The archai of palimpsestic memorization", 2026-01-18. Raw: raw/web-2026-potts-palimpsestic-memorization.md (cited as B, with its section heading). Paper it explains: Rohith Kuditipudi, Jing Huang, Sally Zhu, Diyi Yang, Christopher Potts, Percy Liang (Stanford), "Blackbox Model Provenance via Palimpsestic Membership Inference", arXiv 2510.19796, v1 2025-10-22. Raw: raw/arxiv-2510-19796-blackbox-model-provenance-via-palimpsestic-membership.md (full text of v1 from the arXiv HTML; cited as P, with its section). Potts is a co-author. His post does not add results; it retraces how earlier work on memorisation led to the test, and adds plots the paper does not show: a single-shot memorisation plot on OLMo-2 7B made by Jing Huang (B, "Memorization profiles"), and a checkpoint plot "produced by RJS", the lead authors, "but oddly cut from the final paper" (B, "Seeing the palimpsest").

The claim, plainly

A language model keeps a faint trace of the order in which it saw its training examples: examples seen later get slightly higher likelihood. So whoever trained a model can test whether another model, or a pile of text, came from theirs, by correlating likelihoods with their own shuffled training order; because the order was random, the tests give exact p-values for queried models and for the n-gram text test, approximate ones (from a z-score) for the reshuffle text test (P, Abstract, §1, §3.1–3.3, §4.3.2).

A palimpsest is a page written over many times, with the older writing still faintly visible (P, Definition 1; B, intro).

The road to it, per Potts

Potts builds the idea from three steps found in earlier papers (B, section headings):

  1. Memorised text gets higher likelihood. Carlini et al. (2019) and Prashanth et al. (2024) show memorised strings have lower perplexity and loss; the reverse does not hold, since many high-likelihood strings are not memorised.
  2. Verbatim recall is one end of a range. Memorisation can be rote, rebuilt from context ("reconstruction") or about content rather than strings. Yes-or-no tests of verbatim recall hide near misses.
  3. Each example's trace peaks, then fades. Likelihood of a trained-on string jumps soon after the step that saw it, then drifts back toward the mean (Tirumala et al. 2022; Lesci et al. 2024; Chang et al. 2024). Huang's OLMo-2 7B plot shows a single injected string's loss falling from about 2.74 to 2.48 within about 25 steps, then rising to 2.50–2.55 and staying there over 200 steps; the baseline stays near 2.73–2.76 (B, "Memorization profiles"). The peak comes some steps after exposure; Huang et al. (2024) put this down to Adam's momentum (same section).

The order then shows directly: on Pythia-6.9b-deduped, at each checkpoint the batches it has seen line up by loss in the order they were trained, earliest highest; a model trained on another order shows no such ranking (B, "Seeing the palimpsest"). The paper's Figure 1 shows the same effect another way: it regresses each training example's negative log-likelihood on its training order, for pythia-6.9b-deduped (a clear trend) and for pythia-6.9b, trained on an independent order (near zero) (P, §1, Figure 1).

Potts notes the Pythia paper (Biderman et al., 2023) found memorised sequences spread evenly through training, which seemed to rule this out. He reads that as an effect of its strict verbatim test; the paper finds the trace is stronger, not weaker, on the sequence starts Pythia used (B, "Memorization profiles"); in the paper, sampling from the start of a sequence gives smaller p-values on OLMo-7B (P, Appendix A.1, Figure 5).

The tests (P, §3)

SettingAlice hasStatistic
QueryBob's model, to score textSpearman rank correlation between Bob's log-likelihood of each training example and its training step; optionally minus a reference model's log-likelihood to cancel how predictable each text is (Eq. 1–2)
Observational, partitiononly Bob's textsplit her ordered data into k blocks, build an 8-gram index per block, count matches with Bob's text, correlate counts with block order (Algorithm 2)
Observational, reshuffleonly Bob's textretrain the last 1–10% of her run on fresh shuffles; is Bob's text likelier under her real model than under the copies? (Algorithm 3; a z-score, approximate p-values)

Shuffled training data makes any training-order statistic exactly calibrated under the null: permuting the order gives exchangeable copies (P, Assumption A1, Algorithm 1, Theorem 1). The tests are meant to be powerful, open about their method, and to need no change to Alice's model or data (P, §1).

Key results

  • Small effect, many samples. The correlation itself is tiny, 0.001 to 0.1, but with enough queries p-values get very small; a reference model cuts the queries needed about tenfold (P, §4.2, Figure 2a).
  • Fine-tunes are caught. Over 40 fine-tunes of Pythia and OLMo base models, 1B to 12B: for OLMo, p < 1e-4 in every case and < 1e-13 in all but three; for Pythia, p ≤ 1e-8 in all but four, all at 1.4B. The three that escaped with p > 1e-5 had pretraining loss of 4.5–5.6 against the base model's 3 ± 0.1, so hiding cost them quality (P, §4.2).
  • The trace survives more training. A Pythia checkpoint at step 70,000 trained 70,000 more steps still gives p = 2.23e-66 (P, Appendix A.3). The first-epoch order of OLMo 7B and OLMo-1.7 7B still shows in their final checkpoints, after 544B and 2,300B more tokens (p < 1e-10 and < 1e-4) (P, §4.2). A 3M-parameter TinyStories model trained 10 epochs keeps the order of the last 4 or 5 (P, Appendix A.4). Bigger models keep more (P, Appendix A.3).
  • No logprobs needed. Sampling a model 1, 4 or 16 times per prefix to estimate probabilities still gives p = 1.6e-4, 5.6e-7 and 6.4e-6 on OLMo-7B (P, Appendix A.6). Estimated API cost for GPT-4.1 mini: $179.20 (P, Appendix D.1, May 2025 prices).
  • A side finding. The results "strongly suggest" that pythia-2.8b-deduped was trained on the non-deduplicated Pile (p = 1e-60), against its documentation (P, §4.2).
  • Text alone is harder. The n-gram test on Pythia text needs several hundred thousand tokens for consistently low p-values, so it cannot flag short posts; the authors suggest platform-wide estimates instead (P, §4.3.1, Table 1). The reshuffle test gets p < 1e-3 from 320 tokens on TinyStories, but loses power once Bob fine-tunes on 1% of Alice's data (P, §4.3.2, Figure 3). On OLMo 2 checkpoints, 640 tokens give median p = 6.25e-3 (1B) and 1.44e-3 (7B) (same section).
  • Output similarity misleads. A rival method that compares two models' outputs calls pythia-1.4b and pythia-1.4b-deduped related, though they are independent runs (P, Appendix C).

Caveats

  • Alice needs her training order, and a third party needs to see at least part of it; the authors suggest disclosing a slice, such as the order of Wikipedia documents (P, §5).
  • Distillation largely escapes the test: in pilot runs a signal came only with distillation sets possibly as large as Alice's training data (B, "Looking ahead"; not in P).
  • Potts says hiding a stolen model takes "so much additional work" that theft becomes pointless (B, "Palimpsestic tests"). The paper backs this for fine-tuned models queried directly; for the text-only reshuffle test, fine-tuning on 1% of the data was enough to lose power on TinyStories (P, §4.3.2).
  • Weight edits might hide a model, and models may not all memorise alike; Potts names both as open (B, "Looking ahead").
