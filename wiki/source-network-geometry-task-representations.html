Connectivity geometry shapes how task representations compress and expand across cortex

source created 2026-10-23 updated 2026-10-23 neuroscience fmri representations dimensionality connectivity generalization 2026

Authors: Lakshman N. C. Chakravarthula, Takuya Ito, Alexandros Tzalavras, Michael W. Cole Published: Nature Neuroscience, online 2026-10-22, doi 10.1038/s41593-026-02486-8 (not open access; only its abstract was read) Raw copy: raw/biorxiv-2025-03-14-643366-network-geometry-task-representations.md — the published abstract plus the full text of the bioRxiv preprint, version 3 of 2026-09-03 (CC BY-NC-ND), same authors. The published abstract matches the preprint's except for wording; the published body could not be checked (raw header). Cited as R, with the preprint's section.

Terms

  • Representational dimensionality. How many independent directions a brain region's activity patterns use across 96 task conditions, measured by the participation ratio of the eigenvalues of their similarity matrix. High means conditions look different from one another; low means many share patterns (R, Results, first section).
  • Compression / expansion. A region's dimensionality minus the average of the regions feeding it: negative is compression, positive is expansion (same section).
  • Functional connectivity (FC) and its dimensionality. How strongly regions' resting-state signals co-vary, estimated per subject at the level of cortical vertices; its dimensionality is the participation ratio of the singular values of the source-to-target connectivity matrix. Low means the target's vertices all receive similar mixtures of the sources (same section).
  • Activity flow model. Predict a target region's task activity as a weighted sum of source-region activity, with the resting-state connectivity as weights; a linear, one-step model (R, "Cross-region activity flows…").

The claim, plainly

How a cortical region's task representation changes relative to its inputs follows the dimensionality of its input connections: low-dimensional connectivity compresses, high-dimensional connectivity expands, and a simple linear model that only passes activity through measured connections reproduces much of this (R, Abstract, Results). This explains a pattern found earlier on the same data (Ito and Murray, 2023, with 45 conditions; re-measured here with 96): representations compress from sensory to association cortex, then expand toward motor cortex (R, Results).

Key results

Main data: fMRI from 18 people doing 16 tasks with 96 conditions (the multi-domain task battery), with a replication of the dimensionality pattern in 352 Human Connectome Project (HCP) subjects using 24 conditions over 7 tasks (R, Methods, Results).

  • Compression then expansion (prior finding, re-measured). Dimensionality is high in sensory regions, low in association regions (strongest in default-mode and auditory networks), and high again in motor regions; for the change in dimensionality from sources to target, a U-shape fits better than a line (ΔAIC 3.94) (R, Results, first section). In the HCP replication, 347 of 352 subjects show compression in association regions (R, Results, last section).
  • Connectivity dimensionality predicts the change. Correlation between compression and connectivity dimensionality r = 0.44, holding in sensory, association and motor systems, and r = 0.4357 after regressing out region size, signal quality and reliability (R, Results, first section).
  • The linear flow model reproduces geometry. Predicted task activity correlates with observed at r = 0.16 to 0.83 across the 360 regions; predicted and observed similarity matrices have cosine similarity 0.22 to 0.84; predicted dimensionality correlates with observed at r = 0.55; all beat shuffled-connectivity nulls (R, "Cross-region activity flows…").
  • Transformation, not copying. In 339 of 360 regions, the predicted geometry is closer to the target region's than to the sources' (R, "Activity flows over connectivity patterns drive…").
  • Low-dimensional input makes tasks look alike. Regions with lower connectivity dimensionality show more similarity across tasks (r = −0.26); in a simulation with random source activity, lower connectivity dimensionality gives more cross-task similarity (ρ = −0.988) (R, "Low-dimensional connectivity shapes…").
  • Expansion goes with conjunctions. In an exploratory analysis of 5 tasks, how strongly a region codes combinations of sensory, cognitive and motor features correlates with its expansion (r = 0.46 observed, 0.34 predicted by the flow model) (R, "Activity flow-driven expansions…").
  • Not inevitable. A simulation shows strong nonlinear local computation could decouple connectivity from representational change; the observed coupling puts the brain in between (R, Discussion).

Caveats

  • Correlational fMRI with 18 subjects in the main set; the flow model is linear and uses resting-state, not anatomical, connectivity (R, Methods, Discussion).
  • BOLD signal cannot resolve mixed selectivity below the voxel, which may lower measured dimensionality (R, Discussion). The authors' check: task decoding works throughout cortex, with frontoparietal regions comparable to sensory and motor ones, and compression sits mainly in default-mode and auditory networks, so it is not a general BOLD artefact (R, Results, first section).
  • The conjunction and cross-task analyses are exploratory, on a task set not designed for them (R, Results).
  • In the HCP data (n = 352), higher dimensionality goes with higher fluid intelligence (r = 0.29). The authors resolve the apparent clash with compression: the compression-then-expansion pattern is shared within each person, while absolute dimensionality varies between people (R, Results, Discussion).

For machine learning

This section is the wiki's reading, not the paper's. In a linear layer, the output's dimensionality cannot exceed the rank of the weight matrix, nor the dimensionality of its input: weights whose rows are all alike squeeze many input patterns onto few directions, making different inputs look alike (good for sharing across tasks); varied rows keep the directions they are given. A linear map can only keep or lose dimensions; making feature combinations separable in the ML sense needs a nonlinearity. The paper's "expansion" is measured against the average of single source regions, not all sources pooled, so a target that mixes several low-dimensional sources can exceed each one while staying linear (R, Discussion). The authors themselves note that such constraints "have been extensively characterized in artificial neural networks" (R, Discussion).
