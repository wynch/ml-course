Driven by Compression Progress

Author: Jürgen Schmidhuber (TU Munich; IDSIA) Published: arXiv 0812.4360, v1 2008-12-23, v2 2009-04-15. Essay of 35 pages built on a 2008 keynote and 2007 invited lectures; it distills his curiosity work from 1990 on (R, abstract note). Raw copy: raw/arxiv-0812-4360-driven-by-compression-progress.md (captured 2026-09-25, full text of v2)

Every claim below cites the raw file as R, with the paper's section number.

Summary

An agent should keep its whole history, keep trying to compress it better, and reward itself in proportion to the bits it saves. A reinforcement learner then picks actions that lead to data it can learn to compress: the steepest part of its own learning curve (R, §1.2). The paper's claim is that one rule explains attention, curiosity, novelty, beauty, art, science and jokes as by-products (R, §2).

Two definitions carry the argument (R, §2.3-2.4):

TermDefinitionSection
Subjective beautyHow few bits the observer's current compressor needs for the dataR, §2.3
InterestingnessThe first derivative of beauty over time: the compression progress still to be hadR, §2.4, Eq. 1

Key claims

  • Reject Shannon surprise. A dark room is boring because it is fully predictable; white noise is boring because it can never be compressed. Only data with regularities not yet learned is novel (R, §2.6).
  • Reward the improvement, not the error. The 1990 systems rewarded prediction error; the 1991 follow-up switched to predictor improvement, because error-seeking agents get stuck on noise (R, §3.1-3.2). The 1995 variant used the KL divergence between prior and posterior, later called "Bayesian surprise" (R, §3.3).
  • How to measure progress. Evaluate the old and the new compressor on the same stored history; reward the difference, e.g. C(p_old, h) − C(p_new, h) (R, §A.5-A.6). A cost variant counts one bit saved as worth halving run time (R, §A.4, Eq. 4).
  • Discovery is an unusually large compression breakthrough, such as Newton's law of gravity compressing many falling-object records (R, §1, §2.8).
  • Science and art share the drive. Both seek data that is new but not random; science states the saved bits formally, art may not (R, §2.15).
  • Beyond unsupervised learning. Clustering, factorial codes and prediction are special cases of compression; the missing piece is choosing the data by action (R, §2.9).
  • Hard-wire curiosity. Universal learners could in theory learn it, but at a constant cost too large for bounded agents, so the paper argues it should be built in (R, §1.3).
  • Statistical learners miss algorithmic regularity: the digits of π look random to any windowed predictor although a short program computes them (R, §3.3).

Caveats

  • An essay and a synthesis, not a new experiment. Its evidence is prior implementations (R, §3), one cited human-attention study (R, §3.3) and the author's own drawings and anecdotes (R, §2.14-2.16, §4).
  • It names but does not solve the hard part: computing progress without re-running the compressor on the whole history (R, §5).
  • It sets aside the case where curiosity is harmful, "kill the cat" (R, §1.3, Appendix A).
