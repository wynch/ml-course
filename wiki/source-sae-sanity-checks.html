Sanity Checks for Sparse Autoencoders

Summary

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) rewrites a model's activation as a sparse sum of learned directions, in the hope that each direction is one of the model's own features (R, §2.1). The paper asks whether training actually finds those features, with two tests.

Test 1, known features. The authors build fake activations in 100 dimensions from 3,200 planted feature directions, about 20 active per sample, and train SAEs of the matching size (R, §3.1). BatchTopK and JumpReLU SAEs explain 67–71% of the variance yet recover almost nothing: with equal feature frequencies, 3 of 3,200 features (cosine above 0.8); with long-tailed frequencies, 9% (BatchTopK, 297) and 7% (JumpReLU, 225), all from the most frequent features (R, §3.2, Figures 2–3).

Test 2, random baselines on real models. On Gemma-2-2B (layers 12 and 19) and Llama-3-8B (layer 16), each SAE is compared with three crippled copies: decoder directions frozen at random; decoder held within cosine 0.8 of its random start ("soft-frozen"); encoder frozen at random (R, §4.1). The copies come close on every standard score (R, §4.2, Abstract):

  • interpretability judged by an LLM (AutoInterp): 0.88 soft-frozen against 0.90 trained (BatchTopK); the abstract gives 0.87 against 0.90;
  • sparse probing with the single best latent: 0.69 against 0.72 (abstract); gaps of 0.001 to 0.07 across settings;
  • causal editing (RAVEL): soft-frozen matches or beats the trained SAE, up to 0.78.

Reconstruction is where training shows most: at L0 = 160 the trained JumpReLU explains 0.85 of the variance, the soft-frozen copy 0.79 and the fully frozen ones about 0.6 (R, §4.2).

The authors' reading: SAEs move little from their random start ("lazy training") and the dictionary is so large that some random directions line up with any concept by chance. Reconstruction does not reward matching the model's features, so it is a poor guide to whether they were found (R, §4.2, §6, Appendix D).

Caveats

  • Architecture matters in the toy test. A plain TopK SAE recovers 99.9% of features in the equal-frequency setting and up to 43% in the long-tailed one; the abstract's 9% is for BatchTopK only. The authors call TopK's result an artefact, since its frozen copies also match it on real data (R, Appendix B, Appendix C).
  • Causal editing barely moves anywhere off layer 12. On Gemma layer 19 and Llama-3-8B every variant, trained or not, scores 0.48–0.53 (R, Appendix E, Tables 3–4), so that metric separates little there.
  • The toy features fire independently; real features are correlated (R, §5).
  • Transcoders and crosscoders are not tested (R, §5).
  • The authors present the baselines as checks, "not a verdict on SAEs as a paradigm" (R, §6).
