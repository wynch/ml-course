Synthesis

A representation is the vector a network assigns to an input, z = f(x); the function f is the encoder (L11, slide 22). A deep net makes one per layer (L11, slide 3). A good one "makes a subsequent learning task easier" (Goodfellow et al., quoted in L11, slide 24), which is why representations transfer: train on a task with much data, then freeze f and fit a linear map, or fine-tune f, on a task with little (L11, slides 25–29).

The three lectures give three ways to shape f:

  1. Compress the data (L11). An autoencoder squeezes x through a small code and rebuilds it; k-means and vector quantization squeeze it into one of k codes (L11, slides 37–49).
  2. Predict missing data (L11). Mask part of the input and predict it: colorization, masked autoencoders, BERT. It "often works better than autoencoding", and why is "still an open question" (L11, slides 52–62).
  3. Pull similar inputs together, push others apart (L12). Contrastive learning trains f on pairs; which pairs count as "similar" decides what f ignores (L12, slides 28–46).

Lecture 13 adds a fourth source that needs no training: the architecture itself. With random weights and infinite width, a network is a Gaussian process whose covariance, set by the architecture and nonlinearity, already says which inputs are similar (L13, slides 7, 23).

Lecture 11: Representation Learning I (Isola)

Representations and layers. Each layer is a different representation of the data; going from data to embedding is representation learning, the reverse is generative modeling (L11, slide 3). Common layers act on data as simple maps: scaling, shifting, ReLU, sigmoid, normalizing to unit length, softmax (L11, slides 8–9). Early CNN units respond to edges and textures, later ones to object parts and whole objects, from Zeiler and Fergus 2014 (L11, slides 17–20). An aside shows a small MLP trained with SGD and with steepest descent in the spectral norm separating two classes layer by layer (L11, slide 12; see source-bundle-mit-6-7960-optimizer-geometry).

What makes a representation good. Compact, explanatory, disentangled into independent factors, interpretable, and making later problems easy (L11, slide 31, after Bengio 2013).

Two approaches. Compression (autoencoding, contrastive, clustering) and prediction (future, missing data, pretext tasks); the slide asks whether they are really different (L11, slide 35).

Autoencoders. Encoder f and decoder g minimise E‖x − g(f(x))‖², with a code of size M smaller than the input N (L11, slides 39–40). If both are linear, the code spans the same M-dimensional subspace as PCA (L11, slide 41). In a quick experiment on images of coloured shapes, a probe on deeper layers recovers shape better (about 75% → 99% from layer 0 to 6) and colour worse (about 73% → 58%) (L11, slide 43; read from the plot). The wiki's reading: the code keeps what is needed and drops the rest.

Clustering and VQ. k-means is the same objective with f and g as lookup tables: f maps a point to one of k integers, g maps the integer to its cluster mean, trained by alternating updates (L11, slides 46–48). Make f and g deep nets and you get a vector-quantized autoencoder (VQ-VAE, VQGAN), trained by backpropagation with approximations (L11, slide 49). Isola's framing: clustering is "making up new words for things" (L11, slide 45).

Self-supervised learning. Turn an unlabelled problem into a supervised one by cooking up labels from the data itself, a pretext task (L11, slide 56): predict colour channels from a grayscale image (Zhang, Isola and Efros 2016), the masked patches of an image (MAE), or masked words (BERT) (L11, slides 53, 58–60). On ImageNet classification from frozen features, colorization and an autoencoder are about equal at the first two layers; from conv2 on colorization is ahead, about 33% against the autoencoder's best of about 21% (L11, slide 61; read from the plot). Three hypotheses for why masked prediction wins: a bottleneck is hard to tune and low-dimensional codes optimise badly; autoencoders can copy part of the input and stall there; prediction is closer to the tasks we care about (L11, slide 62).

Lecture 12: Similarity-Based Representation Learning (Beery)

What helps generalization. On CIFAR-10, representations of a network trained on true labels show tight, well-separated classes; with random labels they do not (L12, slides 7–8, from Chuang et al. 2021). Wanted: concentration (same class close), separation (classes apart), robustness to irrelevant changes (L12, slide 9).

Metric learning. Learn a distance in which similar pairs are close and dissimilar pairs far. The linear version learns z = Wx, so distances are Mahalanobis distances with A = WᵀW (L12, slide 12; Xing et al. 2003, slide 13). The deep version learns a nonlinear z = f(x) (L12, slide 16). Losses compare distances: the triplet loss wants an anchor closer to a positive than to a negative by a margin (Schroff et al. 2015); later losses compare against all negatives in a batch (L12, slides 18–21). "Hard" negatives, currently closer than a positive, speed learning (L12, slide 25).

Contrastive self-supervised learning. The encoder maps data onto a unit sphere, and the loss is a softmax cross-entropy that picks the positive out of N + 1 candidates (L12, slides 28–30):

L_cont(f) = E[ −log ( e^(f(x)·f(x⁺)/τ) / ( e^(f(x)·f(x⁺)/τ) + Σᵢ₌₁ᴺ e^(f(x)·f(x⁻ᵢ)/τ) ) ) ]

with τ a temperature. This is a form of InfoNCE (van den Oord et al. 2018), related to noise-contrastive estimation, NCE (Gutmann and Hyvärinen 2010); slide 29 lists both. Without labels, positives are two augmentations of one image and negatives are other data, as in SimCLR, where each of B images gives 2 views and the other 2(B − 1) views are negatives (L12, slides 33–34). Other choices of pair: two colour channels of one scene, two frames of one video, an image and its caption (CLIP) (L12, slides 35–37).

What the loss does.

  • It maximises a lower bound on the mutual information between the two views: MI(f(x), f(x⁺)) ≥ log N − L_cont (Poole et al. 2019; L12, slide 39).
  • It balances alignment, positives close, against uniformity, features spread evenly over the sphere to keep information (L12, slides 40–41). Across 306 STL-10 and 108 BookCorpus encoders, the best downstream accuracy sits where both are low (L12, slide 43, Wang and Isola 2020).
  • The pairs teach invariance: whatever the augmentations change, the representation learns to ignore. Finding the right invariances "can be challenging for different types of data" (L12, slide 45).

Why a sphere: training is more stable, and well-clustered classes on a sphere are linearly separable by cutting off caps (L12, slide 31).

What makes it work. Heavy augmentation, a projection head, large batches (many negatives), and choice of pairs (L12, slide 47). The loss is applied to g(h), a small head on top of the representation h, and h is used downstream; the head helps, "possibly because representation h then need not be completely invariant to augmentations" (L12, slides 49–50). Large batches are expensive; MoCo is more efficient (L12, slide 51). Random negatives sometimes share the anchor's class; debiased losses correct for that (L12, slide 52). With labels, use same-class images as extra positives (Khosla et al. 2020; L12, slide 53).

Where it falls short (Cole et al., CVPR 2022). On iNaturalist 2021, 10,000 species and 2.7M training images, SimCLR with a linear probe trails supervised training by about 30 points at species level (top-1 about 0.50 against 0.80), against a gap of about 7% on ImageNet; the gap is small for coarse groupings and grows as the task becomes more fine-grained (L12, slides 54–68). Slide 68's summary, "On ImageNet, contrastive SSL matches supervised", is looser than slide 64's "Imagenet ~7% gap"; the measured figure is the gap. The wiki's reading: augmentations that keep "dog" intact can erase the small cues that tell two species apart.

Lecture 13: Architectural Bias on Representations (Bernstein)

Aside: Muon. Steepest descent under the spectral norm gives ΔW = −(Tr(Σ)/λ)UVᵀ for G = UΣVᵀ (L13, slide 2). With momentum, low precision and a fast iteration for UVᵀ, this is the "Muon optimizer", which "trains nanoGPT to 3.28 val loss on 'Fineweb' dataset in < 15 minutes" (L13, slide 3; a speedrun claim by Keller Jordan). Here Σ holds G's singular values, not the Gaussian-process covariance Σ(x, x′) used later in the lecture. Tr(Σ)/λ is the step size of plain steepest descent under the spectral norm (slide 2); slide 3 gives no step-size rule for Muon itself. See source-bundle-mit-6-7960-optimizer-geometry.

The claim of the lecture. "A neural architecture (even without training) already expresses an opinion about data similarity" (L13, slide 7).

Two older ways to fit data.

  1. Kernel methods: put a bump k(x, xᵢ) on each data point and fit weights, f(x) = Σᵢ αᵢ k(x, xᵢ); such functions form a "reproducing kernel Hilbert space" (L13, slide 9).
  2. Gaussian processes: a distribution over functions consistent with the data, with closed-form mean and standard deviation at any new point (L13, slides 10, 13, 18). Formally, f is a Gaussian process if every finite set of values [f(x₁), …, f(xₙ)] is jointly Gaussian; the covariance function Σ(x, x′) says how strongly the values at x and x′ move together, so its choice "encodes what we mean by 'nearby'": e.g. e^(−(x − x′)²), or the inner product ⟨x, x′⟩ (L13, slides 15–17).

The correspondence. Sample a network's weights at random and you get a random function (L13, slide 20). For a 3-layer MLP of width 1000 on image inputs, outputs on two nearly identical images are tightly correlated over 1000 random draws, and outputs on an image and a scrambled copy are more loosely correlated; the pairs look jointly Gaussian (L13, slides 21–22). The theorem: with iid weights, as width → ∞, any finite set of network outputs is jointly Gaussian, and the covariance "depends on the architecture and non-linearity" (L13, slide 23). The proof uses the multivariate central limit theorem: each output is a sum of many independent terms from the layer below (L13, slide 24). Condition: infinite width and random iid weights; this describes the network before training.

Example. ReLU MLP with nonlinearity √2·relu and weights drawn from N(0, 1/fan-in): E f(x) = 0 and E f(x)f(x′) = h ∘ … ∘ h (xᵀx′/d), h applied L − 1 times, where h(t) = (1/π)[√(1 − t²) + t(π − arccos t)], the "compositional arccosine kernel" (L13, slide 25). Open: how the covariance depends on architecture and weight distribution, and whether that can guide architecture design or regularization (L13, slide 26). References: Neal; Cho and Saul; Lee, Bahri, Novak et al. (L13, slide 27; none captured).

What each component does, and how the maths ties to it

This section is the wiki's reading of the slides.

  • The encoder's last layer is what a downstream linear probe sees, so "good" means linearly usable: tight classes, far apart (L12, slides 8–9; L13, slide 6).
  • The bottleneck in an autoencoder forces a choice of what to keep; a linear one keeps the top principal directions (L11, slide 41). Masking forces the same choice without a size limit.
  • The softmax in the contrastive loss is a classifier over the batch: the numerator pulls the positive in, each negative's term pushes it out, and τ sets how sharply hard negatives dominate (L12, slides 28, 39). The unit sphere makes the dot product a cosine, so only direction matters.
  • The augmentations are the real specification of the representation: they define which differences count as noise (L12, slide 45).
  • Width and random weights. At random initialisation each unit sums many independent inputs, so outputs become Gaussian; the nonlinearity, applied layer after layer, decides the covariance, here the arccosine kernel h repeated L − 1 times (L13, slides 24–25). Its effect at depth is the low-rank bias of source-bundle-mit-6-7960-approximation-generalization (L6, slide 59).

Open questions

  • Why masked prediction beats autoencoding: three hypotheses, none settled (L11, slide 62).
  • How the random-network covariance depends on architecture, and whether that can guide design (L13, slide 26).
  • When contrastive SSL fails: fine-grained tasks such as iNat21 (L12, slides 64–68).
