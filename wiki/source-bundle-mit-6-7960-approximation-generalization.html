Synthesis

Both lectures use one frame: learning has three parts (L3, slide 4; L6, slide 3).

  • Approximation: is there a network in the family that fits?
  • Optimization: can training find it?
  • Generalization: does it work on data it has not seen?

Lecture 3 takes the first. On the unit cube, a network with three layers (two hidden ReLU layers and a linear output) can match any function whose slope is bounded, to any accuracy in total error, but the number of units it needs grows exponentially with the input dimension (L3, slides 10, 18). Depth changes this: a deep, narrow ReLU network can make a function with 2⁵⁰⁰ − 1 bends that a three-layer network would need a width of about 7 × 10⁴⁹ units per layer to match (L3, slide 33). Bernstein stresses that this says nothing about training or generalization (L3, slide 34).

Lecture 6 takes the third. Networks fit random labels perfectly, so counting the functions they can represent gives no useful bound, yet on real labels they still generalize (L6, slides 42–44). So they must carry other preferences, called inductive biases, that choose among the many perfect fits. Isola lists four: most random weight settings give simple functions; depth favours low-rank representations; the optimizer prefers low-norm and flat solutions; architecture builds in symmetries (L6, slides 50–62).

The wiki's reading: the two lectures meet at the rectangle construction. Lecture 3's proof fits any function with narrow bumps; trained that way, the network would put a bump on each training point and "would not generalise" (L3, slide 19). That is Lecture 6's "filing cabinet" (L6, slides 6, 8). So expressive power is not the question; which fit training picks is.

Lecture 3: Approximation Theory

The question. Given a family of target functions G (with "pathological functions" excluded) and a family of networks F, can some f in F come within ε of any g in G? Error is measured as the worst gap, maxₓ |f(x) − g(x)|, or the total gap, ∫|f(x) − g(x)| dx (L3, slide 7).

Targets. A function is L-Lipschitz if |g(x + Δx) − g(x)| ≤ L‖Δx‖: its slope never exceeds L (L3, slide 8). For d inputs the lecture measures Δx in the RMS norm, √((1/d)Σxᵢ²), the typical size of one entry (L3, slide 9).

Theorem. For any L-Lipschitz g on the unit cube [0,1]ᵈ and any ε > 0, a 3-layer ReLU network with N = 4d(L/ε)ᵈ units gets ∫|f − g| dx < 2ε (L3, slide 10). Conditions: a bounded input domain, a bounded slope, and the total-gap error, not the worst gap.

Proof in three steps.

  1. In one dimension, cut [0, 1] into N strips and use a staircase. On each strip the curve can drift at most L/N from the step, so each strip's error is at most a triangle of area ½·L/N²; the total is at most L/(2N), so N = L/(2ε) strips suffice (L3, slides 12–13).
  2. In d dimensions, use N small boxes of side 1/N^(1/d). The error is at most L/N^(1/d), so N = (L/ε)ᵈ boxes are needed (L3, slide 14). This is where the exponential cost in dimension enters.
  3. ReLUs build boxes. Four ReLUs, relu(cx) − relu(cx − 1) − relu(c(x − 1) − 2) + relu(c(x − 1) − 3), approach a unit-height step on [0, 1] as the slope c grows (L3, slide 15). Add one such step per input coordinate and apply one more ReLU with threshold d − 1: the sum passes d − 1 only where every step is on, which gives a box (L3, slides 16–17). A weighted sum of shifted boxes approximates g (L3, slide 17).

Bernstein's own caveats. It needs exponentially many neurons in d; c → ∞ "feels unrealistic"; approximating rectangles "feels like a trick" (L3, slide 18). If trained, such a network would just put rectangles on the training points, weight decay would suppress the rest, and it "would not generalise" (L3, slide 19). Smoother targets need fewer units (Barron's theorem); two layers are enough in principle (Hornik, Stinchcombe and White 1989) (L3, slide 20; neither captured).

Is universal approximation the point? It is not sufficient: Fourier series, polynomials and "the space of Python programs" can all approximate anything, and we rarely learn with them. Whether it is necessary, the slide leaves open (L3, slide 21).

Width against depth. The case for width: shallow nets are already universal, width runs in parallel, depth is sequential and "leads to 'compound problems'" (L3, slide 24). The case for depth is a depth separation: a function a deep network makes cheaply that a shallow one can only make with exponentially more units (L3, slides 25–26).

The depth separation, by counting bends. A ReLU network computes a piecewise-linear function; a "kink" is a point where its slope changes (L3, slide 27). Adding n functions at most adds their kinks; applying a ReLU at most doubles them, since each linear piece can be cut once where it crosses zero (L3, slides 28–29). For layers of width n, this gives at most (2n)ᴸ kinks after L layers: polynomial in width, exponential in depth (L3, slides 30–31). The bound is reached. The tent map g(x) = relu(2·relu(x) − 4·relu(x − ½)) rises from 0 to 1 and back on [0, 1]; composing it with itself doubles its linear pieces each time (L3, slide 32). Composed 500 times, it has 2⁵⁰⁰ − 1 kinks from 1000 layers of width 2; a 3-layer network needs a width per layer of n ≥ ½(2⁵⁰⁰ − 1)^(1/3), about 7 × 10⁴⁹ units, to match the count (L3, slide 33). This is Telgarsky's separation (2015, 2016) (L3, slide 35).

What it does not say. Not that deep nets are easy to train, nor that they generalise; only something about approximation (L3, slide 34).

Note, 2026-10-23 (replaces a "Contradiction (partial)" line written the same day): slide 39 describes Chinchilla's method (a different learning-rate schedule), not a cause of the gap with Kaplan. For the cause, source-reconciling-kaplan-chinchilla finds the schedule minor and the parameter count primary (raw/arxiv-2406-12907-reconciling-kaplan-and-chinchilla-scaling-laws.md, §4, Table 1). See source-mit-6-7960-scaling-laws-lecture.

In practice. Kaplan et al.'s plots show loss as a power law in compute, data and parameters, and models of 2 or more layers nearly on one curve once embedding parameters are left out (L3, slide 38). Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al.) questioned some of Kaplan's results with a different learning-rate schedule, so to settle width against depth one must "obsess over 'minor details' of the training pipeline" (L3, slide 39; the slide dates Chinchilla 2020, the paper is 2022).

Lecture 6: Neural Network Generalization

Terms. Training minimises the empirical risk, the average loss on the training set; we want low population risk, the expected loss on new data from the same source. Generalization is the gap between them (L6, slide 3).

Two ways to fail. Bad data: a cat-versus-dog classifier trained only on cats (L6, slide 5). Bad models: a "filing cabinet" that stores each training pair and answers zero elsewhere has no training error and no useful prediction (L6, slides 6, 8).

Do deep nets generalize? On ten points, a 3-layer ReLU MLP passes through every point and draws a sensible curve between them (L6, slide 8). Isola's counting test for an LLM: if a model were a filing cabinet, it would need one stored answer for every input it handles, s = p·mⁿ, for random lists of n words from m words that it answers correctly a fraction p of the time (L6, slide 9). For "How many citrus fruits are in this list?" on lists of 10 fruits from 30, GPT-4o was right 5 of 5 times, and the slide puts s at 100 trillion, though its own formula gives 30¹⁰ ≈ 5.9 × 10¹⁴, about 590 trillion, so the slide's figure does not follow from it; for "Draw these fruits", 1 of 8, 12.5 trillion (L6, slides 10–11; the slide labels the setting "n = 30, m = 10"). The test is a handful of prompts by hand, an order-of-magnitude argument.

Complexity measures that fail.

  • Number of parameters. Polynomial fits of degree 1, 3, 20 and 1000: degree 20 swings wildly to fit noise (L6, slides 25–28). Belkin et al.'s double descent: test risk rises toward the "interpolation threshold", where training error first reaches zero, then falls again as capacity grows past it (L6, slide 30). Past that point, more random features give a learned function of lower norm, "smoother solutions" (L6, slide 32). One reading offered: the learned model is "simple" plus "spiky", a predictive part plus a part that only fits the noise at the training points (L6, slide 29, citing Belkin, Rakhlin and Tsybakov 2018). The parameter count also misleads directly: h(x) = 10⁻¹⁰⁰f(x) + (1 − 10⁻¹⁰⁰)g(x) has f's parameters, which barely matter (L6, slide 34).
  • Number of functions (VC theory). If the training set dwarfs the number of candidate functions, training error matches population error with high probability; the slide's assumptions are noise-free data, the true function in the class, and an optimizer that picks any perfect fit at random (L6, slides 36–39). The slide states the bound as √(d/n), with d the number of dichotomies, labelings the class can produce, and n the number of training points (L6, slide 41). Networks can fit random labels, so d = 2ⁿ and the bound is vacuous (L6, slides 42–43). (Standard texts define the VC dimension as the size of the largest set the class can label every way, not the number of labelings; the conclusion, a vacuous bound, holds either way. The wiki's note.)
  • The evidence. Zhang et al. 2017 on CIFAR-10: Inception reaches 100% training accuracy and 89.05% test accuracy with random crops and weight decay, 85.75% with neither; on random labels it still reaches 100% training accuracy and 9.78% test, chance level for 10 classes (L6, slide 44). Explicit regularization helps a few points; it is not what makes generalization possible.

"How should we measure model complexity? … for deep learning, it's still an open question!" (L6, slide 46). Parameter norm is marked "maybe" (L6, slide 33).

Inductive biases: what picks among the perfect fits. The version space is the set of all functions with zero training error; something else must choose (L6, slide 49).

  • Parameter-function map. Most random weight settings give simple functions (Valle Pérez, Camargo and Louis 2019) (L6, slides 50–51).
  • Low-rank bias of depth. For random weights, the similarity matrix between inputs' embeddings, the kernel K, has lower effective rank (a smooth count of how many directions carry most of the matrix's variance) the deeper the network: the peaks move from about 2.15 at depth 1 to about 1.45 at depth 16 (L6, slide 59, Huh et al. 2023). It happens in deep linear networks too, where depth adds no expressive power: "products of matrices tend to be low rank" (L6, slides 54–55).
  • Implicit regularization of optimizers. Weight decay acts like an L2 penalty; starting near zero biases toward low norm, and gradient descent from near zero reaches the minimum-norm solution for linear models; SGD and finite-step gradient descent "converge to 'flat' minima", bouncing out of narrow ones (L6, slide 61).
  • Architecture. Invariances, equivariances, compositionality; convnets apply one local rule everywhere, which is why a pix2pix model trained on edge-to-cat pairs draws an eye wherever it sees an oval (L6, slides 19, 62).
  • Domain constraints built into the model (L6, slide 63).

Closing thought. Solomonoff: the shortest program that fits the data generalizes best, but it is intractable (L6, slides 22, 64). Isola recalls Ilya Sutskever arguing, around 2018, that a finite network is enough: "Anything finite will look small once you have enough data" (L6, slide 65; a remembered conversation, not a result).

What each component does, and how the maths ties to it

This section is the wiki's reading of the slides.

  • The ReLU unit is a hinge: it bends a line once. A layer adds hinges side by side (kinks add); stacking layers folds the input range so that later hinges repeat across every fold (kinks multiply). That is why width buys polynomially many linear pieces and depth exponentially many (L3, slides 28–31).
  • A hidden layer of a shallow net acts as a set of bump detectors, one per region of input space; the output layer is a lookup of heights (L3, slides 15–17). That picture explains both universality and its exponential cost in dimension, and why the proof's rectangle construction, if trained, would memorize rather than generalize (L3, slide 19). It is not a claim about trained shallow nets in general: a trained 3-layer MLP fits ten points with a sensible curve (L6, slide 8).
  • Depth and the representation. A stack of weight matrices multiplies them, and products of random matrices concentrate on few directions; so deeper networks map inputs to lower-rank embeddings before any training (L6, slides 55, 59).
  • The optimizer picks among the perfect fits: low norm from a small start, flat minima from finite steps (L6, slide 61). This ties to concept-optimizers, where the choice of norm sets the step.
